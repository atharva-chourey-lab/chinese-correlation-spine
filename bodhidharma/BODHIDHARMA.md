# Case 16 — Bodhidharma: Who Exactly Was He?

**Build:** Chinese Correlation Spine, correction campaign Case 16 (pre-registered
addendum, 2026-11-17). Method: the spine inverted. Bodhidharma is an "Indian"
founder who exists almost entirely inside Chinese dated records, so he is
treated the way the Bṛhadāraṇyaka project treats its text — stratigraphy by
dated witness, lineage-chart forensics, arithmetic that must close.

**Evidence base and its limits.** This build rests on the primary witnesses as
reported in the standard scholarship (Broughton 1999; McRae; Faure; Shahar
2008) and on dated epigraphic/catalogue facts. Where a detail comes only from
secondary summary and could not be checked against the text itself, it is
flagged in place. Two honest gaps are declared up front: (1) the exact first
dated occurrence of the epithet "blue-eyed barbarian" (碧眼胡) was not pinned
in this build — it is standard by the Song lamp literature and is graded
"attested by 1004, earlier occurrence unverified"; (2) the Baolin Zhuan (801),
which fixed the 28-patriarch list, is lost and known through quotation, so
anything credited to it is one notch softer than an extant text.

**Headline result.** A real meditation master from the western regions was
present in Luoyang between 516 and 526 and dead before 534. Almost everything
else in the famous biography — the 527 arrival, the emperor, the nine years at
Shaolin, the severed arm, the single shoe, and his teacher Prajñātāra — enters
the record between 250 and 500 years after his death.

## 1. Source stratigraphy

Witnesses ordered by the witness's **own** date. A motif's first dated
appearance is its terminus, not its truth.

### 1.1 Yang Xuanzhi, *Luoyang Qielan Ji* (洛陽伽藍記), compiled 547 — the earliest witness

A gazetteer of the monasteries of Luoyang, written about the city as it was
before its destruction. In the Yongning Temple entry, Yang mentions a monk
named Bodhidharma in one breath and moves on. What he says, exactly:

- Origin label: a **"hu person from the Persian country"** (波斯國胡人) —
  *hu* is the generic Chinese term for peoples of the Western Regions /
  Central Asia; the label is "Persian Central Asian," not "Indian prince."
- Where seen: at **Yongning Temple, Luoyang**, admiring its great stūpa.
  Yongning Temple was built in **516** and burned in **534**, so the sighting
  falls inside **516–526** on Broughton's reading (the visit must precede the
  temple's destruction and Yang's account describes the intact temple).
- Age claim: Bodhidharma says of himself, **"I am 150 years old, and I have
  passed through numerous countries."** The 150-year age is therefore the
  *earliest* motif in the entire dossier — present at the first mention, as a
  self-claim, in a source that treats him as a wonder-working foreign monk.
- What is **not** here: no meditation teaching, no disciples, no lineage, no
  Shaolin, no emperor, no India beyond the western-regions label, no teacher.

### 1.2 Tanlin (曇林, 506–574), preface to the *Two Entrances and Four Practices* (二入四行論) — the earliest doctrinal attribution

Tanlin was a near-contemporary (more plausibly a student of Huike than of
Bodhidharma himself). His short preface, preserved in Dunhuang manuscripts
and quoted in the *Lengjia Shizi Ji*, is the first text to attach a teaching
and a pedigree to the name:

- Origin: **South Indian**, **third son of a great king** — the royal pedigree
  enters here, in the generation after the man's death, and already conflicts
  with Yang's "Persian" label and (see below) with Daoxuan's caste.
- Doctrine: the *Two Entrances and Four Practices* is attributed to
  Bodhidharma, with **biguan** (壁觀, "wall contemplation") named as his
  mind-pacifying method. Note carefully: *biguan* here is the name of a
  contemplative method, not yet nine years facing a cave wall at Shaolin.
- Disciples: the first to name any — **Daoyu and Huike**.
- What is **not** here: no teacher of Bodhidharma is named. No Prajñātāra.
  No arrival date. No emperor. No arm, no shoe, no Shaolin.

### 1.3 Daoxuan (道宣, 596–667), *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan* (續高僧傳), compiled c. 645, extended to 667 — the first biography

Daoxuan is a historian working from records, a century after the fact, and
his entry is the first actual biography:

- Origin: **South Indian of Brahmin stock** — flatly different from Tanlin's
  royal (Kshatriya-implying) pedigree. The two earliest biographical notices
  already disagree about who he was.
- Itinerary: he **landed at Nanyue (the Guangzhou coast) during the Liu Song
  period** — i.e., arrival **no later than 479** — and travelled north into
  Wei territory. This is the earlier of the two arrival traditions in the
  sources, and it is the one the arithmetic can sustain (§2).
- Teaching: he transmitted the **four-fascicle Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra** to Huike —
  the Laṅkāvatāra affiliation is fixed here, in the mid-7th century.
- Huike's arm: in Daoxuan, **Huike lost his arm to bandits** (and cauterised
  the stump himself). The self-severing in the snow does not exist yet and
  contradicts the earliest biography when it arrives.
- Death: Bodhidharma died and was **buried by the Luo River** (the burial is
  carried in the Huike material); in Bodhidharma's own entry his end is left
  effectively open. The death necessarily falls **before 534** (the collapse
  of the Northern Wei world Daoxuan describes), with the Heyin massacre of
  528 among the candidate contexts. Secondary summaries of Daoxuan's entry
  also transmit an age figure even larger than 150 ("over 180"); flagged as
  secondary-source detail.
- What is **not** here: no Prajñātāra, no Emperor Wu, no Shaolin, no nine
  years, no shoe.

### 1.4 The lineage is invented: Faru's epitaph (689) and the early-8th-century charts

- **Epitaph for Faru (法如, 638–689), 689:** the first lineage claim of any
  kind — Bodhidharma → Huike → Sengcan → Daoxin → Hongren → Faru. Before
  689, no source arranges these men as a succession at all.
- ***Chuan Fabao Ji* (傳法寶記, Du Fei, c. 710–713):** makes Bodhidharma
  "first patriarch" of a Chinese line.
- ***Lengjia Shizi Ji* (楞伽師資記, Jingjue 淨覺, 683–750; c. 713–716):**
  the lineage chart proper — and note what it actually is: a chart of
  **Laṅkāvatāra masters**, rooted not at Bodhidharma but at **Guṇabhadra**
  (the translator), running Guṇabhadra → Bodhidharma → Huike → Sengcan →
  Daoxin → Hongren → Shenxiu. Jingjue's scheme serves his own teacher
  Xuanze's claim to the succession. It preserves Tanlin's preface — our main
  channel for §1.2. The **mind-pacifying dialogue** ("Bring me your mind and
  I will pacify it") belongs to this early-8th-century stratum.
  **Guṇabhadra is the chart's root; Prajñātāra is not in it.** Jingjue, who
  had every motive to give Bodhidharma an Indian teacher, names none.

### 1.5 The Shenhui stratum and the Song compilations

- **Emperor Wu dialogue** ("What merit have I gained?" — "None whatsoever";
  "Who stands before me?" — "I don't know"): first recorded **c. 758**, in
  an appendix to a text of the Shenhui tradition — over two centuries after
  the supposed audience, in the literature of a faction (Shenhui's) that was
  then fighting a lineage war and needed Bodhidharma staged as the
  anti-establishment founder.
- ***Baolin Zhuan* (寶林傳, 801):** fixes the canonical list of **28 Indian
  patriarchs** ending …Prajñātāra (27) → Bodhidharma (28), and builds the
  Indian biographies to match (§4). Largely lost; known by quotation.
- ***Zutang Ji* (祖堂集, 952):** the first *extant* text with the full
  traditional package assembled: arrival in **527** after a three-year sea
  journey; disciple of Prajñātāra; the Emperor Wu encounter; death aged 150;
  burial on Mt. Xiong'er; **Song Yun meets him in the Pamirs three years
  after the burial, carrying a single sandal**; the tomb is opened and
  contains one shoe. The 527 arrival directly contradicts Daoxuan's Liu Song
  arrival — the later text wins in popular memory, the earlier text wins on
  evidence.
- ***Jingde Chuandeng Lu* (景德傳燈錄, 1004):** the canonical fixing of
  everything: arrival dated to the 21st day of the 9th month of Putong 8
  (**527**); the **reed crossing** of the Yangtze; **nine years wall-gazing
  in a cave near Shaolin**; Huike standing in the snow and **cutting off his
  own arm**; the four disciples graded skin / flesh / bone / marrow (Daofu,
  the nun Zongchi, Daoyu, Huike); **six poisoning attempts**; the original
  name **Bodhitāra**, renamed Bodhidharma by Prajñātāra; the gem test and
  the 67-year prophecy (§4.2). This is the biography the world knows, and it
  was compiled ~470 years after the man's death.
- **Shaolin, epigraphically (Shahar 2008):** a stele of **728** is the
  earliest evidence placing Bodhidharma in residence on Mt. Song; a stele of
  **798** already knows Huike's severed arm (as devotion, replacing Daoxuan's
  bandits). The first *explicit* Bodhidharma–Shaolin Temple association in a
  canonical text is the *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* (1004). The connection is real
  by the 8th century; it is unattested for the first two centuries.
- **Martial arts:** the link is the *Yijin Jing* (易筋經), written in **1624**
  by the Daoist Zining with forged prefaces attributing it to Bodhidharma.
  Debunked by Tang Hao in the 1930s and definitively by Shahar (2008);
  popularised by the *Lao Can* novel serialisation of 1904–1907. The
  Shaolin-kung-fu-founder claim is a 17th-century forgery with a
  20th-century marketing career.

## 2. Lineage-chart forensics

### 2.1 Every node cross-dated

| Node | Dates | Independent cross-date? |
|---|---|---|
| Guṇabhadra | 394–468 | **Yes.** Arrived Guangzhou **435**; translated the Laṅkāvatāra **443** (spine anchor, firm); Saṃyuktāgama 435–443. Death 468 from the biographical/catalogue tradition. The chart's one fully anchored Indian node — anchored by Chinese records of his work *in China*. |
| Bodhidharma | ? | **No independent dates.** All termini come from the witnesses of §1: in Luoyang 516–526 (Yang), dead before 534 (Daoxuan's world). |
| Huike | 487–593 | Partially. Daoxuan attests him as Bodhidharma's disciple and Laṅkāvatāra recipient; death in 593 per the biographical tradition. The implied lifespan of **107 years** is longevity inflation of the same kind as his teacher's 150. |
| Sengcan | d. 606 | **Almost nothing independent.** His historicity as Huike's disciple is the chart's weakest link (Whalen Lai flags it as tenuous). A death year with no life around it. |
| Daoxin | 580–651 | Better: attested in near-contemporary records; the East Mountain community is real. |
| Hongren | 601–674 (some sources 602–675) | **Yes.** Multiple independent notices; his disciple Faru's epitaph (689) is a dated inscription from inside the community. The first node after Guṇabhadra where cross-dating fully works. |

### 2.2 The arithmetic, both arrival traditions

**Tradition (a) — Liu Song arrival (Daoxuan, 645): arrival ≤ 479.**
- If the *Lengjia* chart's Guṇabhadra → Bodhidharma link is a personal
  discipleship, he must reach China before Guṇabhadra's death in **468** —
  so arrival by the 460s, in the first half of the Liu Song window.
- Luoyang sighting 516–526: ✓ compatible. Death before 534: ✓ compatible.
- Cost: if he arrived as a trained master aged ≥ 30 by c. 468, he was born
  ≤ c. 438 and died aged ≥ 96. Tight, but humanly possible.
- **Verdict: sustainable, at the price of a long life.** Note that the
  tradition's own 150-year claim is applied at exactly this seam — the legend
  patches the precise joint where the arithmetic strains.

**Tradition (b) — Liang arrival, 527 (*Zutang Ji* 952 / *Jingde* 1004).**
Collapses three independent times:
1. **The Yang Xuanzhi terminus.** Yang (writing 547) places Bodhidharma at
   Yongning Temple in Luoyang, a temple built in 516 and destroyed in 534;
   the sighting falls in 516–526. A man cannot be sightseeing in Luoyang in
   516–526 and land in the far south in 527. Contradiction: the whole
   tradition-(b) timeline starts *after* the earliest witness has already
   seen him in the north.
2. **The Guṇabhadra joint.** A personal disciple of Guṇabhadra (d. 468) who
   arrives in 527 has a gap of **at least 59 years** between the end of his
   discipleship and his arrival; on any normal training age (disciple
   completing training in his 20s–40s by 468), he arrives aged roughly
   **85–110** and dies (536, traditional) older still. Tradition (b) is
   arithmetically survivable *only inside the legend's own 150-year
   lifespan* — it does not merely prefer the miracle, it requires it.
   (The escape hatch — reading Guṇabhadra → Bodhidharma as succession to an
   office, the Laṅkāvatāra mastership, rather than personal pupilage — saves
   the chart by conceding the point: an office-succession is precisely the
   retrojected lineage label McRae describes, §5.)
3. **The Song Yun anachronism (internal).** The *Zutang Ji* itself dates
   the arrival to 527 *and* tells of Song Yun meeting the sandal-carrying
   Bodhidharma in the Pamirs "three years after his burial." Song Yun's
   mission is independently dated **518–522** (spine anchor). So on the
   legend's own terms the burial falls by c. 519 at the latest — at least
   **eight years before the arrival the same text asserts**. The legend
   borrows a real, dated envoy and wrecks its own chronology doing it.

**Downstream squeeze.** The chart buys continuity with longevity at two
consecutive nodes (Bodhidharma 150, Huike 107). After Huike (d. 593),
Sengcan dies only 13 years later (606), while Daoxin — born 580 — was 13
when Huike died and at most 26 when Sengcan died; his traditional nine years
of service under Sengcan must fall c. 597–606, aged 17–26. Survivable, but
only just, and only because Sengcan is a near-blank. The first *normal*
generational interval in the entire chart is Daoxin (b. 580) → Hongren
(b. 601): 21 years — arriving exactly where documentation thickens.

### 2.3 Where cross-dating stops

The chart has two halves with opposite evidentiary characters:

- **Chinese half (Guṇabhadra's China activity → Hongren):** cross-datable
  from **435** onward, because these men worked inside the Chinese record
  system — catalogues, inscriptions, court history. Guṇabhadra's node is
  firm; Hongren's is firm; the middle is thin but not fabricated wholesale.
- **Indian half (Mahākāśyapa → … → Prajñātāra → Bodhidharma):** cross-dating
  is possible **nowhere**. A handful of its names (Aśvaghoṣa, Nāgārjuna,
  Vasubandhu) are independently datable *people* via spine TAQs (Chinese
  translations by 402–413, by 409, Paramārtha's *Life* respectively) — but
  their sequence positions and teacher–pupil links are unverifiable, and the
  final three names before Bodhidharma (Basiasita, Punyamitra, Prajñātāra)
  are attested **nowhere outside the Chan list tradition at all** (§4).

The seam between the halves is exactly Bodhidharma himself — the one figure
who stands in both worlds and is documented in only one of them.

## 4. Prajñātāra (般若多羅) — the node before him

Atharva's question, verbatim: "Prajnatara?" — the right question. If
Bodhidharma's dossier is thin, his teacher's is thinner by an order of
magnitude, and the thinness is diagnostic.

### 4.1 Attestation sweep

Checked against every pre-801 witness in §1 and against the earlier Chinese
lineage construction:

- **Tanlin (6th c.):** names no teacher of Bodhidharma. Absent.
- **Daoxuan (645–667):** no teacher named. Absent.
- ***Lengjia Shizi Ji* (c. 713–716):** the chart runs Guṇabhadra →
  Bodhidharma. Jingjue needed an Indian pedigree and used the translator
  instead. **Prajñātāra is absent from the very chart this build was asked
  to cross-date** — he enters a *different, later* chart (the 28-patriarch
  list), not the Laṅkāvatāra chart.
- ***Fu Fazang Yinyuan Zhuan* (付法藏因緣傳, presented as a translation by
  Kekaya with Tanyao, 472):** the earlier Chinese patriarch construction —
  23 patriarchs from the Buddha to **Siṃha Bhikṣu**, whose beheading in
  Kashmir is said to **end the transmission altogether**. Prajñātāra is not
  in it; worse, the text's whole point is that there *is* nobody after
  Siṃha.
- **First appearance: the *Baolin Zhuan* (801)** fixes the 28-patriarch list
  with Prajñātāra at 27. The text is lost; the list is known through the
  tradition it standardised. First *extant* narratives naming him as
  Bodhidharma's teacher: ***Zutang Ji* (952)**; first full biography:
  ***Jingde Chuandeng Lu* (1004), ch. 2.**
- **Outside Chan lineage literature: nothing.** No Indian source, no Tibetan
  source (Tibetan patriarch lists follow the Fu Fazang-type line and stop at
  Siṃha), no Central Asian source, no non-Chan Chinese source attests
  Prajñātāra the patriarch. **The negative is explicit: his entire existence
  in the record is inside one genre — Chan lineage literature — and inside
  it only after 801.**
- One namesake caution: a Sanskrit-writing **Prajñātāra of Nālandā** is
  recorded in the mid-**9th** century (three folios preserved in Japan,
  brought by Enchin) — four centuries too late, no connection shown, and a
  warning against name-matching. Philologists have also suggested the
  re-Sanskritisation itself may be wrong (the Chinese 般若多羅 has been read
  as **Prajñādhara**). Even the man's Sanskrit name is a back-formation.

### 4.2 The story layers, dated

| Motif | First dated appearance |
|---|---|
| Name in the list, as 27th patriarch | 801, *Baolin Zhuan* (lost; via the standardised list) |
| Named as Bodhidharma's teacher in an extant text | 952, *Zutang Ji* |
| His own hagiography: East-Indian Brahmin orphan, childhood name **Keyura** ("Precious Necklace"), recognised by Punyamitra (26th) as an incarnation of Mahāsthāmaprāpta | 1004, *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* ch. 2 (presumably Baolin-derived) |
| **The Kāñcī gem test:** King Xiangzhi ("Most Fragrant") of South India gifts Prajñātāra a priceless jewel; Prajñātāra tests the three princes with it; only the youngest, Bodhitāra, answers that a worldly jewel cannot illuminate itself and is surpassed by the Dharma-treasure | 1004, *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* (the gem-test story is cited to *Jingde* ch. 2). Keizan's *Denkōroku* (c. 1300–1320s) renames the king **Siṃha** and supplies the brothers' names (Candraprabha, Guṇaprabha) — the story is still growing in the 14th century |
| **The renaming:** "Dharma means great penetration; you shall be Bodhidharma" | 1004, *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* ch. 27 |
| **The prophecy:** "Remain in South India. After my nirvana, wait **sixty-seven years**, then go to China… When you go, **do not dwell in the south** — they delight only in worldly achievements" (with a calamity verse) | 1004, *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* ch. 27 |
| His death at 67, ascending into the sky and bursting into flame, raining relics | 1004, *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* |
| Prophecies attributed to him by still later patriarchs: the 574–577 persecution (why Huike and Sengcan hid in the mountains); the future rise of Mazu Daoyi | Song lamp-record attributions — the dead patriarch keeps issuing prophecies as later centuries need them |

Note the engineering in the 67-year prophecy. It is reverse-fitted: it
exists to weld the Indian half of the chart to the Chinese dates — stay in
India a precisely counted interval, then arrive on schedule. Modern
devotional tradition completes the weld by supplying Prajñātāra's death as
457 CE — and 457 + 67 = 524, inside the traditional arrival window. A
prophecy whose numbers only work backwards is not a prediction; it is a
patch, of the same kind as the 150-year lifespan at the Guṇabhadra joint.

### 4.3 The Indian patriarch list: construction and the seam

- The **Fu Fazang** list (472) gives **23** patriarchs, Buddha → Siṃha, and
  closes the line with Siṃha's execution. Tiantai adopted the same 23 and
  appended its own Chinese masters (Huiwen, Huisi, Zhiyi).
- Chan needed the line *open* — Bodhidharma had to receive something in
  India to bring to China. The **Baolin Zhuan (801)** therefore (i) inserts
  Vasumitra into the early list (23 → 24), and (ii) **reopens the line the
  Fu Fazang had declared closed**, supplying three new names after Siṃha:
  **Basiasita (25), Punyamitra (26), Prajñātāra (27)** — then Bodhidharma
  (28).
- Competing Tang attempts to bridge the same gap disagree with each other
  most exactly at the bridge: the *Lidai Fabao Ji* (c. 775) offers its own
  resolution, Zongmi's (780–841) handling differs again, and modern analysis
  of the Tang lists (the "Missing Link" study of Siṃha's fate) confirms the
  names between Siṃha and Bodhidharma are the least stable segment of the
  entire list — minor variation for patriarchs 1–23, maximal variation for
  24–27.
- **Does Prajñātāra's position survive contact with the earlier list? No.**
  He is not a figure the earlier list lost; he is an insertion at the seam,
  one of three names first attested precisely where the chart needed an
  Indian teacher for Bodhidharma and the inherited source said the line was
  extinct.

### 4.4 The gender question

- **Pre-modern record:** every Chinese source places Prajñātāra inside an
  all-male patriarch list; classical Chinese frequently leaves gender
  unmarked, and no pre-modern text or identified depiction presents him as a
  woman. The Jingde biography's details (orphan, Keyura, Mahāsthāmaprāpta
  incarnation) carry no female marking.
- **The female identification is modern.** It was proposed in print by Rev.
  Koten Benson (**2008**), on three grounds: claimed oral traditions in
  Korean Seon and in Kerala identifying Prajñātāra as female; the name
  (Prajñā + Tārā, both female deities); and archaeological evidence for
  famous female teachers in south India generally (real, but not evidence
  about this person). Practitioner literature adds a claim that Dōgen's
  *Shōbōgenzō* ch. 50 refers to Hannyatara as female — not verified against
  the text in this build and not accepted in mainstream scholarship.
- **Grade: modern conjecture (2008).** Mainstream scholarly reaction ranges
  from dismissal to "possible but unevidenced." The honest statement is
  narrower than either camp's: the pre-modern record does not establish his
  gender because it barely establishes *him*; a tradition that cannot
  document the person cannot be overturned on the person's sex.

### 4.5 Prajñātāra — verdict rows

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Historical person | **Unproven; default = lineage construction at the seam.** Zero attestation before 801, zero attestation outside Chan lineage literature ever, inserted at the exact point where the inherited list (472) said the transmission had ended |
| Bodhidharma's teacher | **Late accretion** (801/952 vs. Bodhidharma's death ≤534: a gap of ~270–420 years) |
| Floruit | **None independent.** He inherits Bodhidharma's arithmetic entirely: *if* Bodhidharma (fl. c. 480–530) had an Indian teacher, that teacher's floruit falls c. 440–490 — arithmetic on a hypothesis, not a date. If the Guṇabhadra link is office-succession rather than pupilage (§2.2), Prajñātāra need not even be Guṇabhadra's contemporary |
| The gem test, renaming, 67-year prophecy | **Hagiography, first extant 1004** — narrative machinery for welding the chart's two halves |

## 5. Modern scholarship map

- **Broughton (1999, *The Bodhidharma Anthology*):** translates the earliest
  stratum (the Dunhuang *Two Entrances* materials and Tanlin's preface) and
  treats it as the closest recoverable core: a real meditation master in the
  Luoyang region in the early 6th century, around whom an "anthology" of
  sayings crystallised. His dating of the Luoyang sighting (516–526) is the
  load-bearing terminus of §2.
- **McRae (1986; *Seeing Through Zen*, 2003):** the lineage is retrospective
  construction — his maxim is that lineage assertions are "as wrong as they
  are strong." The patriarch chart is ideology in genealogical form, and
  Bodhidharma functions as a textual figure assembled over centuries by the
  factions that cited him.
- **Faure (1991; 2003):** Bodhidharma's historical existence is "dim"; the
  figure is a rhetorical and hagiographic construction — down to the body
  itself (the blue eyes, the barbarian otherness) — successively rebuilt by
  Chan factions, Song editors, and modern national traditions.
- **Ferguson (2000, *Zen's Chinese Heritage*):** the tradition's own voice —
  retells the received biography with caveats flagged; valuable as a record
  of what the lineage believes about itself, not as critical history.
- **Dumoulin:** accepts a historical South Indian monk of the 5th–6th
  century behind the legend; doubtful of the Persian label; treats the
  wall-contemplation reputation as rooted in the man's actual practice
  profile.
- **Japanese scholarship:** Yanagida Seizan's Dunhuang source-criticism
  reconstructed early Chan from the manuscripts and exposed the Laṅkāvatāra
  school milieu that manufactured the first charts; Sekiguchi Shindai
  pressed radical doubts about the *Two Entrances* attribution itself. The
  terms of the modern debate were set earlier, in the 1930s Hu Shih – D. T.
  Suzuki exchange: Hu Shih read the texts as datable documents of a
  historical movement; Suzuki defended the tradition's reading. Suzuki's own
  historical judgement is worth quoting against the tradition he defended:
  the 28-patriarch scheme was constructed when Chan, grown popular, was
  attacked for having "no transmission" — the pedigree answers a polemic.

**Consensus paragraph.** Modern scholarship agrees on the shape of the
answer even where it splits on the man: a historical meditation master from
the western regions — most plausibly South Indian by the earliest
biographical layer, Persian/Central Asian by the earliest witness of all —
was active in north China in the first decades of the 6th century, taught a
contemplative method associated with the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra, gathered at
least two named disciples, and died before 534; everything beyond that
kernel — the royal birth, the imperial audience, the Shaolin decade, the
severed arm, the empty tomb, the Indian teacher, and the 28-patriarch line
itself — was deposited in dated layers between the 7th and the 11th
centuries (and, for the martial arts, the 17th) by a tradition building its
own pedigree. The split is over the kernel's size: Broughton and Dumoulin
keep a recoverable teacher at the centre; McRae and Faure treat even the
centre as mostly textual effect. Nobody working from the dated sources
defends the received biography, and nobody needs Bodhidharma to be a
fiction — the Chinese record is quite sufficient to have contained the man;
it is simply insufficient to contain the legend, which is how we know the
legend is later.

## 6. Verdict

| Question | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Did the man exist? | **Yes — high confidence.** Two independent near-contemporary witnesses (a non-sectarian gazetteer, 547; a disciple-line preface, 6th c.) with no shared motive to invent him, plus a sober first biography (Daoxuan) whose errors are the ordinary kind |
| When did he flourish? | **c. 480–530 CE.** Hard points: present in Luoyang **516–526** (Yang Xuanzhi via Yongning Temple's 516 construction / 534 destruction); dead **before 534**. Arrival in China best supported under the **Liu Song (by the 470s)** per Daoxuan — the only arrival tradition compatible with the Guṇabhadra joint and the Luoyang terminus. The famous **527 arrival is falsified** — by ~1–11 years against Yang, by ≥59 years against Guṇabhadra's death, and by ~8 years against its own text's Song Yun story |
| Where was he from? | **Western Regions — best-supported answer: South India, confidence moderate; Persian/Central Asian, confidence live.** The *earliest* witness says Persian *hu*; the next two say South Indian. The spine rule (the record closest to the event governs what it directly records) keeps the Persian label permanently on the table; the South-Indian reading wins on the weight of the biographical tradition, not on priority. Royal birth: unproven. Pallava prince: modern conjecture, no Indian-side evidence |
| Which famous stories are early? | The 150-year age claim (as a claim — 547); wall contemplation as a *method name* (6th c.); the Laṅkāvatāra affiliation and Huike's discipleship (by 667); residence on Mt. Song (by 728) |
| Which are later charters for the lineage? | Emperor Wu dialogue (758 — Shenhui's faction); Prajñātāra and the 28-patriarch line (801 — pedigree under polemical pressure); the 527 arrival, single sandal, empty tomb (952 — Five Dynasties consolidation); nine years at Shaolin, self-severed arm, reed crossing, poisonings, gem test, 67-year prophecy (1004 — Song canonisation); martial arts (1624 — forgery) |
| Prajñātāra | **Lineage construction at the seam** (§4.5). No attestation before 801, none outside Chan lineage literature, inserted where the 472 list had closed the line |

**What evidence would change the verdict.**
1. **Any pre-801 attestation of Prajñātāra** — a Dunhuang fragment, a
   catalogue entry, a Tibetan translation naming him as Bodhidharma's
   teacher — would move him from "construction" to "tradition with an early
   root," and force re-grading of the whole Indian half of the chart.
2. **Any Indian-side attestation of Bodhidharma** — an inscription, a
   Sanskrit or Tamil text, a Pallava-era record of a prince-monk departing
   for China — would transform the origin question overnight and give the
   Indian half of the chart its first cross-datable node.
3. **A dated 6th-century Chinese document naming Bodhidharma's teacher,
   origin, or arrival year** (a tomb inscription, a monastery record, a
   dated colophon) would collapse the floruit interval to a point and
   arbitrate the Persian-vs-South-Indian conflict with evidence instead of
   witness-weighting.
4. **Recovery of the *Baolin Zhuan* text** would let the 801 layer be read
   instead of inferred, and would date (or dissolve) the gem-test and
   prophecy motifs currently credited to 1004 by default.
5. Conversely, a pre-534 source placing a *different* master behind the
   *Two Entrances* attribution would shrink the historical kernel toward
   McRae's and Faure's end of the consensus — the verdict's confidence in
   the man rests on two witnesses, and it is honestly held at exactly that
   strength.

# THE MEDICINE FILE — the Ayurvedic canon through the correlation web

*Built 2026-11-17, on Atharva's flag that traditional medicine had had no
update from the day's dating-correction programme. Method: the Chinese
Correlation Spine (see ../README.md) — every traditional date for the
medical canon tested against foreign dated records: Chinese catalogues,
pilgrims and annals; Arabic bibliographies and compendia; Tibetan
translation colophons; and dated physical manuscripts. Verdict classes
are the Correction Campaign's: CORRECTED / CORROBORATED / BOUNDED /
NO BITE. A foreign record dates the event it directly records — a
translation, a catalogue entry, a sojourn — and constrains the Indian
original only as a bound, never as a composition date.*

The structural finding first, because it organises everything below:
**the great saṃhitās travelled west, not east.** Caraka and Suśruta
were translated into Arabic in Baghdad and quoted there by 850 CE; no
Chinese bibliography ever records a translation of either, and the
Tibetan canon took only Vāgbhaṭa's epitome. What reached China was
Ayurveda as *practice and formulary* — Jīvaka-attributed prescriptions,
an Indian eye treatise, a massage method, court physicians — carried
inside Buddhist and commercial traffic, not the books themselves. The
dating evidence follows the same split: the canon's hard foreign
anchors are Arabic; China's anchors date the *system's presence and
prestige*, and one of them (Yijing, 691) turns out to be the sharpest
bracket on Vāgbhaṭa that exists anywhere.

---

## 1. Caraka Saṃhitā

**The tradition.** Caraka, redactor of Agniveśa's tantra, was court
physician to Kaniṣka. The text's own colophons give the layering
plainly: Agniveśa composed; Caraka redacted; Dṛḍhabala of Pañcanadapura
restored the lost final third (the last seventeen chapters of the
Cikitsāsthāna and the whole Kalpa- and Siddhisthāna) centuries later.

**What the Kaniṣka bound does to it.** Campaign 1 (Case 2) confined
the Kaniṣka epoch to **c. 125–165 CE**, excluding the old 78 CE
equation. Taken at face value, the tradition therefore no longer puts
Caraka in the 1st century: it puts the *redaction layer* in the
**mid-2nd century CE** — which is, not coincidentally, exactly where
the text's independent internal window already sits (present form
2nd–5th c.; Dṛḍhabala fl. 300–500). The bound corroborates the
scholarly window; it does not prove the identification, for three
reasons the file must state:

- The tradition's only early carrier is itself foreign and late: the
  Chinese *Sūtrālaṃkāra* (大莊嚴論經, T.201), translated by
  Kumārajīva's bureau **c. 402–413**, where a physician named Caraka
  attends Kaniṣka alongside Māṭhara and Aśvaghoṣa (Lévi, 1896). The
  story is attested ~250 years after the event it claims.
- "Caraka" may name a school or a professional type (the wandering
  physician-teacher) rather than one man; the Indian tradition itself
  also made him an incarnation of Śeṣa and identified him with
  Patañjali — the name attracts identifications.
- Even if the identification holds, it dates one *layer*. The
  Agniveśa core behind it is older by an amount no record measures.

**Foreign TAQs for the text.** The Arabic route is the hard one.
ʿAlī ibn Rabban al-Ṭabarī's *Firdaws al-Ḥikma* (**c. 850**) devotes
the closing section of its seventh part to a 36-chapter summary of
Indian medicine drawn, per Meyerhof's analysis, from four named
books: **Charaka (Jarak), Suśruta (Susrud), the Nidāna, and the
Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya**. A full Arabic Charaka existed to be summarised by
850; Ibn al-Nadīm's *Fihrist* (**987**) lists Sharaka among the Indian
medical books translated in the Barmakid-era movement (the Charaka
translation is credited to ʿAbdallāh ibn ʿAlī, 9th c., via Persian).
Al-Rāzī's *Ḥāwī* (c. 900) quotes Sharaka as a routine authority.

**Chinese trace — thin, and honestly so.** No Chinese translation of
the Caraka Saṃhitā is recorded in the Sui catalogue or any Tang
bibliography. The name reaches China only as Kaniṣka's courtier in
the story collection above. One indirect content trace: Sun Simiao's
*Qianjin Yaofang* (**652**) quotes "the great Indian physician
Jīvaka" — *there is nothing in the world that cannot be used as
medicine* — a line Deshpande traces to Caraka Saṃhitā, Sūtrasthāna
26.12, arriving under Jīvaka's name, not Caraka's.

**Tibetan transmission.** No complete Tibetan translation of the
Caraka Saṃhitā exists in the Tengyur. The canon that Tibet actually
absorbed was Vāgbhaṭa (§3).

**Tightest defensible window.** Core layers c. 100 BCE–200 CE; Caraka
redaction, *if* the Kaniṣka identification is granted, c. 125–165;
Dṛḍhabala's completion 300–500; **present form by c. 500**, with the
formulary tradition already excerpting Caraka-line material in the
Bower Manuscript (c. 450–550, §3). Hard foreign TAQ: **850** (Arabic).
**Verdict: BOUNDED** (Kaniṣka tradition), **CORROBORATED** (existence
and present form well before the Arabic TAQ). Strongest foreign
anchor: the *Firdaws al-Ḥikma*, c. 850.

## 2. Suśruta Saṃhitā

**The tradition.** Suśruta, son of Viśvāmitra, learned medicine from
Divodāsa, king of Kāśi — a frame that would put the text in the Vedic
age. Nothing foreign, epigraphic or physical reaches a historical
Suśruta; the frame is a teaching lineage, not a date. **Verdict on
the Vedic-antiquity claim: NO BITE.**

**The layered text** (Meulenbeld's assessment, the scholarly
baseline): composition began in the **last centuries BCE**; an old
five-book version was redacted and the long sixth book, the
Uttaratantra, added by a later hand; the work was known to Dṛḍhabala
(fl. 300–500), giving the **present form by c. 500 CE**. The oldest
surviving manuscript — Kathmandu KL 699, palm-leaf, dated **878 CE** —
already contains the complete six-book text, Uttaratantra included:
a physical TAQ for the finished form.

**The Nāgārjuna-as-redactor tradition, tested.** Manuscript colophons
and medieval commentators name a redactor Nāgārjuna; the 1952
Chronology Committee of the National Institute of Sciences of India
placed "the recension of the Suśruta Saṃhitā by Nāgārjuna" in the
**3rd–4th centuries CE**. Campaign 1 (Case 4) bounded the Mādhyamika
Nāgārjuna to a floruit of **c. 150–250**, hard TAQ 409. Three grades
follow:

- *If* the redactor were the Mādhyamika, the redaction would date to
  c. 150–250 — inside the text's growth period, so not absurd on
  chronology alone.
- But the identification itself is the weak link: Meulenbeld's
  assessment is that the 2nd-century-Nāgārjuna redaction **has been
  disproved**; the name Nāgārjuna attaches to several later figures
  (the alchemical Nāgārjuna of the rasaśāstra tradition among them),
  and the committee's own 3rd–4th-century placement already concedes
  it is not obviously the Mādhyamika.
- Even a genuine 2nd- or 3rd-century redaction would not date the
  present text, whose Uttaratantra layer runs later (complete by
  c. 500).

**Verdict: the identification — NO BITE** (unprovable, and on current
philology rejected); **the text — BOUNDED**: core from the last
centuries BCE, present form by c. 500.

**Foreign TAQ.** The Arabic *Kitab-i-Susrud* is the canon's cleanest
translation anchor: the *Fihrist* records that **Mankha (Manka), an
Indian physician at the ʿAbbāsid court, translated Suśruta from
Sanskrit for Yaḥyā ibn Khālid al-Barmakī** — Yaḥyā fell in **803**, so
the translation falls in **786–803**. Mankha is independently placed
at Hārūn al-Rashīd's court (he is remembered for curing the caliph).
By 850 the *Firdaws* is summarising Susrud alongside Charaka.
**Strongest foreign anchor: Mankha's translation, 786–803** — Suśruta
existed, complete enough to translate whole, by 803 at the latest,
some three centuries after the internal evidence already closes its
present form. Like all translation TAQs it confirms; it does not
tighten.

## 3. Vāgbhaṭa, the minor saṃhitās, and the Bower Manuscript

### Vāgbhaṭa — Aṣṭāṅgasaṅgraha and Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya

The scholarly window is **c. 600–650** (Meulenbeld: c. 600; estimates
run 550–650; whether one Vāgbhaṭa wrote both works is itself
disputed). The foreign records bracket it from three sides, and one
of them is the single best dating instrument in this whole file:

- **Yijing, writing in 691** (*Nanhai Jigui Neifa Zhuan*, vol. 3,
  §27, "On understanding the origin of disease"): the eight branches
  of Indian medicine "formerly existed in eight books, but **lately a
  man has compiled them into one fascicle**" — and Yijing describes
  the resulting single-volume epitome as the current state of the
  art. The standard scholarly reading (from Takakusu's translation
  onward) identifies the unnamed compiler's epitome with Vāgbhaṭa's
  Aṣṭāṅga synthesis. The Chinese does not name him, so the
  identification is graded probable, not firm — but a dated
  eyewitness in India in 673–685 reporting the eight-branch epitome
  as *recent* is precisely a floruit bracket: the synthesis existed
  by 691 and was new enough to remark on. It fits c. 600–650 and
  presses against anything much later.
- **Mādhava's Nidāna (c. 700)** borrows verses from the
  Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya wholesale (Indian-internal evidence), and the
  *Firdaws* (850) already summarises the Hṛdaya in Arabic — the
  "Ashtankar" of the *Fihrist* (987) is most plausibly this same
  Aṣṭāṅga work (or its twin, the Saṅgraha) in Arabic dress. The
  identification of "Ashtankar" specifically has been debated; after
  the *Firdaws* attestation it is corroborative, not load-bearing.
- **Tibetan translation**: the Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya is the one great medical
  saṃhitā the Tibetan canon took — Tengyur, Toh 4310, with
  Candranandana's commentary — translated in the later-diffusion
  enterprise associated with Rinchen Zangpo (958–1055). A TAQ of
  c. 1055: real, but the loosest of the three brackets.
- From below: Vāgbhaṭa epitomises Caraka and Suśruta in their
  Dṛḍhabala-era forms, so he stands after c. 500.

**Tightest defensible window: c. 550–650**, with Yijing (691,
"recently compiled") as the forcing foreign record at the top and
the post-500 state of his sources at the bottom. **Verdict:
CORROBORATED** — the one canon author whose traditional scholarly
window the foreign record actively tightens rather than merely
permits.

### Bhela Saṃhitā

Bhela, fellow-pupil of Agniveśa under Ātreya, survives in a single
fragmentary Tanjore manuscript. No Arabic, Chinese or Tibetan
attestation of the book exists. What dates the *tradition* is
physical: the **Bower Manuscript's medical parts excerpt Bhela-line
material** (below), so a Bhela formulary tradition existed by
c. 450–550. The present text is layered on older material and is
commonly placed in the 6th–7th centuries in surviving form.
**Verdict: BOUNDED** (tradition by c. 500; present text later and
only loosely placed). Strongest anchor: the Bower Manuscript.

### Kāśyapa Saṃhitā (Vṛddhajīvakīya Tantra)

The paediatrics tantra taught by Mārīci Kāśyapa, composed by
Vṛddhajīvaka and redacted by Vātsya — surviving, like Bhela, in a
single incomplete manuscript (Kathmandu). Traditional datings (6th
c. BCE for the tantra) have no foreign or physical support; scholarly
placements put a core in the early centuries CE, Vātsya's redaction
later (often c. 7th c.), with possible retouching later still. No
foreign record names the text. **Verdict: NO BITE** — the web does
not reach it; its dates remain an internal-Indian argument.

### The Bower Manuscript — the physical anchor

Found in a ruined stūpa near Kucha in 1890: seven birch-bark
treatises in Gupta script, three of them medical — a medical fragment
(Part I), the **Navanītaka** formulary (Part II), and a second medical
fragment (Part III). Dated **palaeographically to c. 450–550**
(Sander: 500–550; Callewaert: c. 450; Hoernle's final view 4th–5th
c.); no radiocarbon date has been published. What it proves by its
date:

- A Sanskrit *formulary* tradition — planned in sixteen chapters,
  opening with a recipe that also stands in the Caraka Saṃhitā, and
  drawing on Caraka-, Suśruta- and Bhela-line material — was mature,
  portable, and being copied on the northern Silk Road by c. 500.
- It is a TAQ not for any one saṃhitā's final form but for the
  *system behind them*: the eight-branch medicine the pilgrims
  describe was already a bookish, excerptable corpus in the Gupta
  age.

**Verdict: CORROBORATED** as an anchor (physical, dated by script;
probable, not firm, because palaeographic).

## 4. The spine biting directly — Indian medicine inside dated Chinese records

**Faxian, Pāṭaliputra, c. 405–411** (*Foguo Ji*). The earliest dated
eyewitness to organised Indian medicine: in the capital, "the
Vaiśya elders and householders have established houses for
dispensing charity and medicine. The poor and destitute, orphans,
widowers and childless, the maimed and cripples, and the diseased
come to these houses; the physicians examine their diseases; they
get food and medicines as needed, and all at no cost." This is an
*infrastructure* anchor: by c. 405–411, institutional, charitable,
physician-staffed medical provision existed in a major Indian city —
the social footprint of the system the saṃhitās describe, observed
within Dṛḍhabala's own floruit window. It dates no text; it dates
the world the texts served. **Firm.**

**The Sui shu bibliography (compiled 641–656).** The 經籍志 lists,
among the medical titles of the imperial collection, a shelf of
Indian works in Chinese dress — the spine's direct hit on Indian
medical *literature* in China:

- 《婆羅門諸仙藥方》 *Pharmacopeia of the Brahmin Ṛṣis*, 20 juan
- 《婆羅門藥方》 *Brahmin Pharmacopeia*, 5 juan
- 《龍樹菩薩藥方》 *Pharmacopeia of Nāgārjuna Bodhisattva*, 4 juan
- 《西域諸仙所說藥方》 *Pharmacopeia Spoken by the Ṛṣis of the Western
  Regions*
- 《西域名醫所集要方》 *Essential Formulas Collected by Famous
  Physicians of the Western Regions*, 4 juan
- 《耆婆所述仙人命論方》 *Formulas on Fate Expounded by Jīvaka*, 2 juan
- 《乾陀利治鬼方》 *Gāndhārī Formulas for Treating Demons*, 10 juan

— eleven Indian medical titles in all by the usual count, most
inherited from the Liang imperial catalogue. What existence-by-656
proves: a substantial Indian formulary literature had been
*translated or compiled in Chinese* by the 6th–7th centuries —
generic Brahmin and ṛṣi pharmacopeias, a Nāgārjuna-branded drug
book (the alchemical/medical Nāgārjuna, note, not evidence about the
Mādhyamika), and Jīvaka material. What it does not prove: the
presence of Caraka or Suśruta in China. Neither is listed, here or
anywhere. **Firm as a catalogue; the titles are lost, so it dates a
transmission, not their contents.**

**Xuanzang, 646** (*Da Tang Xiyu Ji*, juan 2): the standard Indian
curriculum is the five sciences — grammar, crafts, **cikitsā-vidyā
(醫方明, the science of medicine)**, logic, and inner science.
Medicine as a formal, teachable śāstra in India, attested by a dated
eyewitness at the height of the Gupta-successor age. **Firm.**

**Sun Simiao, *Qianjin Yaofang*, 652.** Indian material inside the
greatest Chinese formulary of the age, under Indian names: formulas
attributed to Jīvaka (including the Jīvaka myriad-disease pill), the
Jīvaka saying traced to Caraka Sū. 26.12 (§1), transliterated drug
lore (Agada pill, Kāmalā for jaundice), and a whole regimen — the
**"Brahmin method" of massage and the eighteen postures of Tianzhu**
(天竺按摩法 / 婆羅門十八勢) — filed as Indian health practice.
Dated Chinese attestation that Indian formulary and body-practice
material was circulating in China by **652**. **Firm.**

**Su Jing's *Xinxiu Bencao*, 659** — the imperial Tang pharmacopoeia
records a **"Brahmin's prescription"** (婆羅門方) as the known cure
for beriberi, in all probability carried by an Indian Buddhist monk
(Deshpande). An Indian formula, official in China by 659. **Firm.**

**Nārāyaṇasvāmin at the Tang court, 648–649.** The annals (*Jiu Tang
Shu*, Tianzhu account; *Zizhi Tongjian*) record that Wang Xuance's
returning mission brought the Magadhan brahmin Nārāyaṇasvāmin
(那羅邇娑婆寐), who compounded a longevity drug for Taizong under
imperial patronage — the emperor died in 649, and the annalists let
the elixir hang in the air next to the death. Gaozong's reign adds
the East Indian monk-physician Lokāditya (盧迦逸多), dispatched to
gather rare drugs from the southern seas. Whatever the pharmacology,
the *prestige* is the datum: in 648 the Tang throne imported an
Indian physician for the most sensitive commission in medicine.
**Probable** (annalistic, and the elixir story is told with a moral).

**Yijing, 691.** Beyond the Vāgbhaṭa bracket (§3), Yijing's Record
(§§27–29 of vol. 3) is the fullest dated foreign description of
Indian medicine as practised: the eight branches named; diagnosis by
examination; fasting as first therapy; Indian drugs compared with
Chinese ones item by item; the rule that a physician who has
mastered the eight branches may practise and be honoured. He also
practised medicine himself on the voyage communities. Indian
medicine in 691, from a resident eyewitness: a complete, book-based,
state-recognised profession. **Firm.**

**Wang Tao, *Waitai Miyao*, 752.** The Tang official formulary
(6,900 prescriptions, sources named) is saturated with the Indian
material at one further remove: remedies attributed to Jīvaka
throughout; and its eye section (juan 21) opens with the **Preface
to the Indian Treatise on Eyes** (《天竺經論眼序》) by Master Xie of
Longshang, who says he received the Indian eye-doctrine from a
Western monk — followed by the first detailed Chinese account of
the **golden-scalpel cataract operation** (金篦術), the couching
procedure of the Suśruta tradition. A large share of all
Indian-derived prescriptions preserved in Chinese literature is
concentrated in this one dated book (Fan's count). **Firm** — Indian
ophthalmology existed as a *text* in China by 752.

**Liu Yuxi and the Brahmin eye-doctor, c. 836–837.** The poet Liu
Yuxi (772–842), himself medically literate, suffered three years of
failing sight and addressed a poem to his surgeon: 《贈眼醫婆羅門僧》,
"Presented to the Brahmin Monk Eye-Doctor" — "the master has the
art of the golden scalpel; how shall it lift my blindness?" A
companion poem of winter 837 celebrates the cure ("the golden
scalpel is no longer needed"). An Indian oculist, practising the
signature operation of the Suśruta tradition on a named, datable
Chinese patient, in the 830s. **Probable** (the poems are datable
only through Liu's biography and the 837 companion piece).

## 5. The headline claim — "Ayurveda is 5,000 years old"

The claim dissolves, under the web, into three different claims with
three different oldest defensible dates:

**(a) Medical practice in India.** Genuinely ancient and genuinely
undatable as a system. Drilled molars at Neolithic Mehrgarh
(c. 7000 BCE) show interventionist dentistry on the subcontinent
nine thousand years ago; nobody can call it Ayurveda without
smuggling the later system into a nameless practice. *Oldest
defensible date: none — practice, not a system.*

**(b) The Atharvavedic healing stratum.** The Atharvaveda's healing
hymns — against fever (takman), worms, poison, fracture, with plant
lore and the figure of the healer (bhiṣaj) — belong to the AV's
composition horizon, conventionally **c. 1200–800 BCE**. That is a
healing-*hymn* stratum: no tridoṣa theory, no eight branches, no
saṃhitās. *Oldest defensible date for this layer: c. 1000 BCE,
± two centuries — roughly 3,000 years, not 5,000.*

**(c) The systematised canon.** The eight-branch, tridoṣa system of
the saṃhitās: layers from the **last centuries BCE**, Caraka and
Suśruta in present form by **c. 500 CE**, excerpted on the Silk Road
by c. 500 (Bower), described complete by Yijing in 691, translated
in Baghdad by 803–850. *Oldest defensible date for the system:
c. 200 BCE — roughly 2,200 years; for the texts as we have them,
about 1,500–1,800 years.*

**Grade: NO BITE as stated.** In its usual form — the *system*, the
*canon*, is 5,000 years old — the claim overshoots the systematised
canon by some 2,800 years and the Atharvavedic stratum by some
2,000. What survives correction is still substantial: a three-
millennium documented healing tradition, two millennia of it
systematic. The 5,000-year figure is not an error of detail; it is
the conflation of (a), (b) and (c) into a single birthday none of
them has.

**What would change the grades.**
- *Caraka–Kaniṣka:* an Indian-side attestation of the court-physician
  story independent of the Buddhist story literature (an inscription,
  a Kuṣāṇa-era colophon) would raise it from BOUNDED to CORROBORATED;
  a dated pre-Kuṣāṇa manuscript of the Caraka redaction would kill
  the identification outright.
- *Suśruta–Nāgārjuna:* a manuscript of the five-book Suśruta with a
  colophon naming its redactor and a date, or any pre-500 quotation
  naming Nāgārjuna in that role, would reopen the identification.
- *Vāgbhaṭa:* a Chinese or Tibetan witness that *names* the epitomist
  Yijing leaves anonymous would make the 691 bracket firm.
- *Bower:* a published radiocarbon date for the leaves would convert
  the file's central physical anchor from probable to firm — and
  move every bound that leans on it.
- *The canon in China:* a Dunhuang or Turfan fragment of an actual
  Caraka or Suśruta translation would overturn §4's structural
  finding; the catalogues say none was ever registered.

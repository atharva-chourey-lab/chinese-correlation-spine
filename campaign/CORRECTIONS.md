# Correction Campaign — results

Run 2026-11-17 against `campaign/CAMPAIGN-PREREG.md` (15 cases declared
in advance, including two calibration cases and one deliberate
expected-NO-BITE control). Method and grading follow
`~/workspace/brihadaranyaka-dating/research/CHINESE-SPINE.md`: the
Chinese record governs the event it directly records; a colophon gives
a terminus ante quem (TAQ) for the Indian original minus an unknown
lag (empirical lag: median ≈ 160 yrs, 80% interval ≈ 35–400 yrs —
spine §4); a pilgrim dates what he saw and heard in the years he was
there, nothing upstream.

**Verdict classes:** CORRECTED (the Chinese record changes the
accepted answer) / CORROBORATED (independently confirms it) /
BOUNDED (narrows one side only) / NO BITE (does not reach — including
apparent corroboration that turns out not to be independent).

**Headline:** of 15 cases — 5 CORRECTED, 3 CORROBORATED, 5 BOUNDED,
2 NO BITE. The two NO BITEs are the control working as designed: a
campaign in which the Chinese record bit everywhere would be a
campaign whose grading could not be trusted.

**Biggest correction:** Case 9, Kashmir. The Tang annals move the

exactly 31 by the 720 investiture — and the modern dates of
Candrapīḍa (712/13–720) and Lalitāditya (724–760), the most famous
king of early-medieval Kashmir, are built on the Chinese pegs, not on
the *Rājataraṅgiṇī*. (Case 8, Harṣa's death, is the larger single fact
but was a declared calibration case, already landed in the spine
catalogue.)

**Where the Indian estimate survived untouched:** Case 15 (Śaṅkara —

## Case 1 — The Buddha's death and the "dotted record"

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** Three competing numbers,
each resting on a different kind of guesswork. (i) The Sri Lankan
traditional date, 544/543 BCE, from the Dīpavaṃsa/Mahāvaṃsa king
lists. (ii) The "corrected long chronology," **483 BCE** (older
computation) — the Ceylon chronicles state that Aśoka was consecrated
**218 years** after the parinirvāṇa; Geiger showed the pre-Aśokan
regnal sums contain a 60-year inflation, and with Aśoka's consecration
at c. 265–268 BCE (Greek-anchored: Rock Edict 13's named kings) the
interval gives 483–486 BCE. The 218 figure itself is a chronicle
sum, and the segment of the king list it spans (Second Council to
Aśoka) contains demonstrably constructed reign lengths. (iii) The
"short chronology": every *non*-Theravāda Vinaya recension — preserved
in Chinese and Tibetan translation — puts Aśoka's consecration only
**100 or 110 years** after the parinirvāṇa, giving c. 368–378 BCE;
the Bechert symposium (Göttingen, 1988) converged on a death
**within a few decades of 400 BCE**, weighing the short interval, the
constructed look of the 218, and the archaeology of second
urbanisation. The honest statement of the Indian-side position: the
Aśoka peg is firm, the interval is the whole argument, and both
intervals come from monastic tradition, not record.

**The Chinese record.** The dotted record (*zhusheng dianji*) is
preserved in Fei Changfang's *Lidai Sanbao Ji* (597 CE) and referred
to by Daoxuan in the *Da Tang Neidian Lu* (664). Its content: after
the parinirvāṇa, Upāli — who had recited the Vinaya — marked **one
dot** beside the Vinaya text at the pavāraṇā ending the first rains
retreat, and a dot was added every year thereafter. The custodianship
passed down a named lineage: Upāli → Dāsaka → Soṇaka → Siggava →
Moggaliputta Tissa → Caṇḍavajjī, and onward with the Vinaya's
transmission. Saṅghabhadra — the Indian monk who translated
Buddhaghosa's *Samantapāsādikā* into Chinese — spent the rains of
**489 CE** (Yongming 7, Southern Qi) at Canton and placed the
**975th dot**. The count then stops in the record.

**The derivation.** If dot 1 was placed in the year of the
parinirvāṇa itself, dot 975 falls 974 years later: 489 CE − 974 =
**486 BCE**. This is numerically identical to the corrected long
chronology run with the modern Aśoka date (268 + 218 = 486). That
identity is the case — see the verdict.

**Verdict: NO BITE (dependent corroboration).** The dotted record
*looks* like an independent Chinese witness converging on the Ceylon
number from the other side of Asia. It is not independent. The
custodian chain it names is, name for name, the **Theravāda Vinaya
lineage** of the Dīpavaṃsa (Upāli through Moggaliputta Tissa is the
Ceylon elders' list); its last custodian is the Chinese translator of
the Theravāda's own Vinaya commentary; and the record survives only
in Chinese catalogues of 597/664 reporting a Sinhalese-lineage
practice. Two copies of one tradition's arithmetic agreeing with each
other is one witness, not two — the record cannot arbitrate between
the lineage it belongs to (486) and the rival interval (~400),
because its number *is* the lineage's number. Pachow's classic study
made the dependence concrete: to land the count exactly on the
already-favoured 483, the 975 dots had to be massaged to 972 ("three
extra dots inadvertently added") — a count that can be adjusted by
±3 to fit the target is a count that was always liable to
retro-fitting. The endpoint is also textually unstable in
transmission (a variant strand counts the dots in 535 with the
practice said to have stopped 53 years earlier, i.e. 482 — yielding
493 BCE by the same arithmetic). Finally, the symposium that set the
~400 consensus had the dotted record in front of it (Bechert's
volumes treat it explicitly, following Pachow) and discounted it on
exactly these grounds; and the genuinely Chinese-transmitted evidence
in this case — the unanimous 100/110-year interval of the
non-Theravāda Vinayas, which reached us *through* Chinese translation
— points the other way. **Firmness:** the record's existence and
content, Firm as a record; its independence, failed. **Strongest
objection to this verdict:** Gombrich's defence — a year-by-year
ritual count is a *practice*, not a computation, and practices are
harder to retro-fit wholesale than king lists; if the dots were
really added annually by a continuous lineage, the record is a
genuine second channel and 486 should stand against the short
chronology. The campaign's answer: the practice's custodians are the
same institution that produced the 218-year sum, so even a perfectly
honest count only re-measures the Theravāda tradition's own starting
epoch — the one quantity in dispute.

## Case 2 — The Kaniṣka era epoch (78 / 127 / 144 CE)

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** Kaniṣka's inscriptions are
dated in an era he founded, years 1–23 (his own) and onward to year 98
under his successors — but the era's epoch is nowhere stated in an
Indian source. The candidates are pure scholarly construction:
**78 CE** (the old equation of the Kaniṣka era with the Śaka era,
long textbook doctrine), **127 CE** (Falk, from the Yavanajātaka's
astronomical epoch and the era's relation to the Śaka era), **144 CE**
(Ghirshman's line). The choice moves the whole Kushan century and
everything hung on it — Gandhāran art, the Mahāyāna sūtra horizon,
Aśvaghoṣa (Case 3). This is the purest guesswork case in the campaign:
three numbers, each an inference chain, none an attestation.

**The Chinese record — the negative, verified.** (i) The *Hou
Hanshu*'s Western Regions account (ch. 88), whose Kushan information
derives from reports reaching the Han court down to c. 125 CE, gives
the dynastic sequence only as far as Kujula Kadphises (Qiujiuque, who
unified the five Yuezhi principalities and took Kabul/Gandhāra) and
his son Yangaozhen (Vima Takto), who conquered India and installed
generals. **Kaniṣka is not named, and no Kushan regnal date of any
kind is given.** Later Chinese histories repeat this material without
adding an era. (ii) Kaniṣka appears in Chinese only inside Buddhist
translation-era literature (the council legend, the Aśvaghoṣa
biography tradition) — as a Buddhist royal personage, never with a
reign date. (iii) Song Yun (520) describes the Kaniṣka stūpa at
Puruṣapura as a standing monument — evidence for the tradition's
footprint four centuries on, none for the era. The spine catalogue's
negative is confirmed on a full pass: **no Chinese source dates
Kaniṣka or his era.**

**The Chinese record — the successor bound.** What the annals do
date is the dynasty's far end. The *Sanguozhi* (ch. 3) records, under
**Taihe 3 = 229 CE** (a minority of secondary literature reads 230),
that "the king of the Da Yuezhi, **Bodiao** 波調, sent an envoy to
present tribute," and the Wei emperor granted him the title "King of
the Da Yuezhi Intimate with Wei." Bodiao is the standard transcription
identification of **Vāsudeva I** — the last great Kushan, whose
era-dated inscriptions run from era year 64 to year 98.

**The derivation.** Vāsudeva I is on the throne in 229 CE, and his
reign overlaps era years 64–98. Therefore era year (229 − epoch) ≥ 64,
so **epoch ≤ 165**; and if his reign ended near his last inscription
and the 229 embassy falls within or just after it, epoch ≈ 229 − 98
to 229 − 64, i.e. **c. 131–165**. Test the candidates: epoch **78**
puts the 229 embassy in era year 151 — requiring Vāsudeva I (or an
unattested namesake succession) to be ruling half a century after his
inscriptional footprint ends and the great-Kushan line closes; the
*Hou Hanshu* horizon cuts the same way from below (a Kaniṣka already
reigning before c. 125 would be expected in the c. 125 report stream
that names his grandfather's generation). Epoch **127** puts the
embassy in era year 102, four years past the last inscription —
comfortable. Epoch **144** puts it in year 85, mid-inscription-range —
also comfortable. The Chinese frame therefore excludes 78 and is
consistent with both survivors.

**Verdict: BOUNDED.** Chinese evidence confines the epoch to
**c. 125–165 CE** (lower edge soft — it rests on the *Hou Hanshu*
report horizon; upper region from the Bodiao entry) and eliminates
the oldest textbook candidate, but it cannot separate 127 from 144,
and the campaign does not pretend otherwise. **Firmness: Probable.**
**Strongest objection:** the whole bound hangs on Bodiao = Vāsudeva
**I**. If Bodiao is a later namesake (a Vāsudeva II has been proposed
for exactly this entry), the 229 date detaches from the year 64–98
inscriptions and the upper bound dissolves; and the *Hou Hanshu*
silence is an argument from a report stream whose India coverage is
thin — silence in it is weak evidence about a king in Puruṣapura.

## Case 3 — Aśvaghoṣa's floruit

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** India preserves
Aśvaghoṣa's works (Buddhacarita, Saundarananda) and a tradition making
him Kaniṣka's court poet — but no date. His floruit is a dependent
variable of Case 2: c. 80–150 CE on the 78 epoch, c. 100–200 on the
127 epoch. Left to Indian evidence alone he floats with the era.

**The Chinese record.** Two dated events. (i) Kumārajīva's Chang'an
bureau translated the **Biography of Aśvaghoṣa** (T.2046) c.
**405 CE**, and works under his name (the Sūtrālaṃkāra) in the same
bureau years — by 405 Aśvaghoṣa was already a *biographised* figure,
with the legend accretion (the converted courtesan, the converted
king) that takes generations to grow. (ii) Dharmakṣema translated the
Buddhacarita itself (T.192) c. **412–421 CE** — a hard TAQ for the
poem in a finished Sanskrit form.

**The derivation.** The 421 TAQ alone, run through the spine's lag
distribution (median ≈ 160 yrs, 80% interval 35–400), gives a
composition window centred in the 3rd century — but the biography
datum tightens it: a text is translated within decades of
composition; a *saint's life* is not. A figure already legendary by
405, whose biography a court bureau bothers to translate, belongs at
least a century earlier — floruit **no later than c. 300, in practice
c. 100–200** — which is also exactly where the Kaniṣka relation puts
him on either surviving era candidate (127/144). The Chinese record
thus *supports the relation* the Indian tradition asserts loosely
(spine §6.3) and converts it from legend into a bounded placement,
without being able to cash it to a decade.

**Verdict: BOUNDED.** Late side hard at 421, working floruit
**c. 100–200 CE**; early side still era-dependent (Case 2's open
127-vs-144 question moves him within that window). **Firmness:
Probable** (the TAQs are Firm; the legendary-maturity inference is
one step beyond record). **Strongest objection:** the biography's
legendary colour is not a clock — hagiography can accrete fast around
a famous name, and if the T.2046 life is substantially a Chinese-era
construction its "maturity" dates the construction, not the man; on
that reading only the bare 421 TAQ survives and Aśvaghoṣa could in
principle be a 3rd-century author whom tradition parked at Kaniṣka's
court.


## Case 4 — Nāgārjuna's floruit

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** Indian and Tibetan
tradition gives Nāgārjuna a lifespan of 400–600 years, makes him the
contemporary of a Sātavāhana king (the Suhṛllekha correspondence), and
scatters him across half a millennium accordingly. Modern scholarship
places him c. 150–250 CE — but that placement is itself substantially
*derived from* the Chinese record below; what Indian evidence alone
supplies is a name, a corpus, and legends.

**The Chinese record.** Kumārajīva's bureau — a court institution
whose output is dated by the Chang'an record — translated the
Mūlamadhyamakārikā with Piṅgala's commentary (*Zhong lun*, T.1564) in
**409 CE (Hongshi 11)**, and Nāgārjuna's biography (T.2047) in the
same bureau years, c. 405. Note the doubleness: the Chinese record
carries the marvels (the biography already has the alchemy and the
nāga-realm recovery of the Prajñāpāramitā) *and* the bureau date. The
campaign's rule is that the date governs.

**The derivation.** A dated translation of the root text by 409,
minus the lag distribution for a text of this profile (the spine's
table gives the MMK an observed lag of ~160–260 years against the
standard composition range), yields the working floruit
**c. 150–250 CE** — and, what matters for the verdict, it *closes*
the tradition's open side: no placement of Nāgārjuna in the 4th–6th
centuries, and no literal reading of the lifespan legends, survives
409.

**Verdict: CORRECTED.** The correction is entirely the catalogue's
(spine §6.6): an Indian author whom his own tradition left floating
across 600 years is pinned to a two-century window by a foreign
translation bureau's dated output. **Firmness: Firm** on the closing
side (the 409 colophon is a court-documented event); the floruit
inside the window remains scholarly placement. **Strongest
objection:** the correction disciplines only the late side. The
early side — whether the MMK's author is the same man as the
Suhṛllekha's author and the alchemist of later tradition (the
"multiple Nāgārjunas" problem) — is untouched by the Chinese record,
and if the corpus is composite, "Nāgārjuna's floruit" is partly a
question about a name, not a man.

## Case 5 — Asaṅga and Vasubandhu

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** India preserved the works
and almost no life-data. The pair's placement (Asaṅga the elder
brother, Vasubandhu the younger) has been given as Asaṅga c. 300–350
/ Vasubandhu c. 400–480 (Frauwallner's "younger Vasubandhu"),
Takakusu's 420–500, Peri's born-c. 350 — a 200-year scatter resting
on relative-order arguments (who cites whom) with no absolute peg on
the Indian side at all.

**The Chinese record.** Three layers. (i) **Paramārtha's *Life of
Vasubandhu*** (T.2049), composed c. 563–569 by an Indian author whose
own dates are Chinese-recorded, survives *only in Chinese* — it is
the primary source for the Puruṣapura origin, the brother relation,
and the death at Ayodhyā aged 80. (ii) Firm translation TAQs for the
works: Vasubandhu's Daśabhūmika commentary translated by Bodhiruci
c. **508–512**; the Abhidharmakośa translated by Paramārtha himself
**563–567**, and again by Xuanzang 651–654; Asaṅga's
Mahāyānasaṃgraha already translated by Paramārtha in 563, the
Yogācārabhūmi by Xuanzang 646–648. (iii) The *Life*'s internal
chronology — Vasubandhu dying "more than 1,100 years after the
nirvāṇa," his teachers placed in round centuries after the Buddha —
which is **tradition arithmetic, not record**: run against any
parinirvāṇa date it collides with the translation TAQs of layer (ii),
which is precisely how we know it is arithmetic.

**The derivation.** The TAQ stack bounds the pair from above: a
commentarial corpus mature enough for Bodhiruci to translate by
c. 510 puts Vasubandhu's floruit no later than the later 5th century
on any plausible lag; Paramārtha — writing within living transmission
of the tradition — treats him as a figure of the recent scholarly
past, not of antiquity. From below, the pair must postdate the
Sarvāstivāda scholastic world the Kośa systematises and the
Nāgārjuna-to-Maitreya lineage Asaṅga's corpus presupposes. The
Chinese frame therefore holds the standard modern placement
(Asaṅga c. 300–400, Vasubandhu **c. 350–500**, panel window
[350, 550]) and excludes both a 3rd-century and a 6th-century
Vasubandhu — while supplying, in the *Life*, the very biographical
scaffolding (brother, birthplace, death-place) that Indian tradition
never recorded.

**Verdict: BOUNDED.** The Chinese record converts a free float into
a two-century frame and preserves the only biography — but the frame
is a bound, not a fix, and the *Life*'s own numbers must be refused
even as its facts are used. **Firmness: Probable** (TAQs Firm; the
*Life*'s factual content one inferential step from record).
**Strongest objection:** Frauwallner's two-Vasubandhu hypothesis was
built from tensions *inside* this same Chinese-preserved material
(the Kośa author's relation to the Yogācāra works); if there were
two Vasubandhus a century apart, the TAQ stack bounds the younger
and the *Life* conflates them — and the campaign's "pair in one
frame" would be an artefact of the biography's conflation rather
than a finding.

## Case 6 — Dignāga and Dharmakīrti

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** Dignāga: c. 480–540
(Frauwallner's landmarks construction), with a live revisionist
proposal of c. 430–500 (Deleanu). Dharmakīrti: c. 600–660
(Vidyabhusana/Frauwallner) against c. 550–610 (Balcerowicz, from
Jaina discussions) and c. 550 (Krasser, accepted by Steinkellner and
Eltschinger). Tibetan tradition adds a legendary layer (Dharmakīrti
as Kumārila's nephew) that no one defends as chronology. The notable
fact for this campaign: **both ends of the standard bracket were
built from Chinese witnesses in the first place** — this case tests
whether the bracket survives its own audit.

**The Chinese record.** For Dignāga, hard data: his corpus was
already curriculum material when Xuanzang studied logic in India in
the 630s–640s, and Xuanzang translated the *Nyāyamukha* in **649**
(the *Ālambanaparīkṣā* followed from his bureau in 657; the spine
catalogue's shorthand "Ālambanaparīkṣā translated by Xuanzang 632"
should be read as his in-India study period — his translation
activity is 645–664 — and is flagged here as a catalogue correction).
Yijing retranslated Dignāga material and Dharmapāla's
Ālambanaparīkṣā commentary around 700. No work of Dharmakīrti was
translated into Chinese in the Tang at all. For Dharmakīrti, the
entire Chinese evidence is the famous pair: **Xuanzang (in India
629–645), who reports the logical scene in detail, never mentions
him; Yijing (writing 691) lists him among the "recent" masters** and
reports his works being taught near Nālandā c. 665–670 (spine C28).

**The derivation.** Dignāga: TAQ 649 firm, effective TAQ in the 630s
(classical status by Xuanzang's student years); with the relative
order Vasubandhu → Dignāga → Dharmapāla (d. 561) the standard
480–540 stands, and the Chinese record neither needs nor permits a
move — it *underwrites* the frame. Dharmakīrti: fame by 691 (Yijing,
positive) and obscurity-or-absence in 629–645 (Xuanzang, negative)
generate the 600–660 convention directly — Vidyabhusana's and
Frauwallner's dating *is* this pair of Chinese data points. The
revisionist c. 550 does not contradict any Chinese *record*; it
re-weights the silence, citing Dharmakīrti's own statement that he
went long unrecognised (which makes Xuanzang's silence compatible
with an earlier floruit) and a possible reference to him in
Dharmapāla's commentary — a passage surviving only in Yijing's
Chinese translation, itself graded unreliable. The Chinese frame
thus fixes the outer bound (famous by 691; not famous in 644) and
leaves the internal fight to Indian and Jaina evidence.

**Verdict: BOUNDED.** The bracket is real but it is a bracket: the
Chinese record bounds Dharmakīrti to [notoriety after c. 645,
established by 691] as a *reception* fact, and any floruit in
c. 550–660 remains compatible with it once the silence is priced as
an argument from silence. **Firmness: Probable.** **Strongest
objection:** the ex silentio half of the bracket is, in the SEP's
words, "short of conclusive — hardly more than one piece in the
puzzle": Xuanzang's interests were Yogācāra-scholastic, his Indian
itinerary did not cover Dharmakīrti's southern milieu, and a master
can be major and unmentioned. If the silence is worth nothing, the
Chinese contribution to this case shrinks to a single TAQ (fame by
691) and the "Chinese-built bracket" framing overstates it.

## Case 7 — Bhartṛhari *(calibration case — spine C26, §6.1)*

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** Tradition makes the
grammarian Bhartṛhari, author of the Vākyapadīya, the brother of a
legendary Vikramāditya of Ujjain — a placement that, taken literally,
drags him toward the 1st century BCE/CE; left as legend, it leaves
him undated. Scholarly placements from relative arguments (his
relation to Dignāga) have run from the 5th to the 7th century.

**The Chinese record.** Yijing, *Nanhai jigui neifa zhuan*, sent to
China in **691** after a decade at Nālandā, names Bhartṛhari's works
(the Vākyapadīya at 3,000 ślokas, the Mahābhāṣya commentary, the
*Pei-na*), makes him a contemporary of Dharmapāla, and states:
**"It is forty years since his death."**

**The derivation.** 691 − 40 = **death c. 651–652 CE**. Max Müller
called the datum "a new sheet-anchor in the chronology of Sanskrit
literature."

**Verdict: CORRECTED** — the calibration standard for the campaign:
a dated eyewitness-generation report overrides a legendary genealogy,
and the burden of proof sits permanently on whoever would move it.
**Firmness: Firm as a record of what Yijing reports; Probable as
history** (the Frauwallner-line pushback — a 5th–6th-century
Bhartṛhari argued from the Dignāga relation, with Yijing's man
demoted to a later namesake — is documented in the spine and keeps
the grade at Probable; the campaign's working placement is death
651–652, floruit c. 600–652). **Strongest objection:** the
two-Bhartṛhari escape hatch cannot be closed from the Chinese side —
Yijing dates the Bhartṛhari *he* means, and if the tradition had
already fused two grammarians of the name, the anchor pins the wrong
man to the right date.

## Case 8 — Harṣa's reign and death *(calibration case — spine C19, C21, C22, §6.5)*

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** Harṣa's accession is
Indian-dated (606 CE, the Harṣa era of his inscriptions). His death
is **not dated by any Indian source**: Bāṇa's Harṣacarita breaks off
mid-reign, the inscriptions stop, and later tradition is silent.
Textbook "d. 647" is not an Indian datum at all — it is this case's
Chinese result, so thoroughly absorbed that its provenance is
forgotten.

**The Chinese record.** A dated annalistic sequence: Harṣa's own
embassy reaches Chang'an in **641 (Zhenguan 15)**; the Tang reply
mission (Li Yibiao, with Wang Xuance as deputy) is in India 643–645
and Xuanzang is received at Harṣa's court, the Kannauj assembly
falling in **643**; Wang Xuance's second mission, dispatched **647**,
arrives to find **Harṣa dead** and a usurper (Aruṇāśa) on the throne,
is attacked, and defeats the usurper in **648** with Tibetan and
Nepalese troops, bringing him captive to Chang'an.

**The derivation.** Alive and ruling in 641–643 (his own embassy,
Xuanzang's reception); dead before Wang's 647 mission reached
Magadha. **Death: 647 CE.** Combined with the Indian accession, the
reign becomes the two-sided bracket **[606, 647]** — the tightest
early control in the BU benchmark panel.

**Verdict: CORRECTED** — in the strongest form the campaign knows:
the Chinese record does not adjust an Indian date, it *supplies* one
that Indian history simply does not contain. **Firmness: Firm** (the
embassy sequence is court annal; only the usurper's name/identity is
Probable). **Strongest objection:** the annals were compiled
centuries after the events (Jiu/Xin Tang shu), and the 647 mission's
narrative survives partly through excerpted fragments of Wang's own
lost account — a single-source chain for the campaign's firmest
verdict; the defence is that the chain is annalistic, internally
dated at every step, and uncontradicted by any Indian evidence.


## Case 9 — Kashmir's Kārkoṭa kings vs Kalhaṇa

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** Kashmir's early-medieval
chronology rests on Kalhaṇa's *Rājataraṅgiṇī* (c. 1148–1150) — a
12th-century verse chronicle whose regnal years, summed from its
chosen epoch, place the Kārkoṭa founder Durlabhavardhana's line and
his grandsons a generation too early: on the received chronology
Candrapīḍa reigns **680–688**, and Lalitāditya's 36-year-7-month
reign falls at **699–736** (Stein's computation from Kalhaṇa's
figures). Kalhaṇa wrote four centuries after these kings, from
genealogies and lost chronicles; his pre-Kārkoṭa books are inflated
by centuries, and even his Kārkoṭa sums are an unverified
transmission of regnal years converted through a mis-set era epoch.

**The Chinese record.** The Tang annals' Kashmir entry (Xin Tang
shu, Western Regions) is a dated diplomatic file: **713 (Kaiyuan
1)** — an embassy from Candrapīḍa (Zhentuoluobili) reaches Xuanzong's
court asking aid against the Arabs and Tibet; **720 (Kaiyuan 8)** —
the Tang court invests Candrapīḍa by edict as King of Kashmir;
**722 (Kaiyuan 10)** — an edict praises Kashmir for provisioning
Tang troops stationed in Gilgit during the Tibetan war. After
Candrapīḍa, the entry records his younger brother **Muduobi**
sending the envoy Wuliduo to request investiture for himself,
offering troops and a temple by the Mahāpadma lake (Wular); Stein
dates this embassy after the Tang Baltistan operations of 736–747.
A second Chinese peg stands at the dynasty's other end: Xuanzang was
in Kashmir **631–633**, a dated residence against which the reigning
king's identity can be checked.

**The derivation.** Candrapīḍa is alive, reigning, and receiving
investiture in **719/720** — but Kalhaṇa's chronology has him dead
by 688. Cunningham's classic computation: the error is "not less
than twenty-five years," and by the 720 investiture **exactly 31
years**. Muduobi = Muktāpīḍa is, in Stein's judgement, beyond doubt
(the name, the brother-sequence Candrapīḍa → Tārāpīḍa → Muktāpīḍa,
and the Mahāpadma detail all lock), so Lalitāditya's embassy and
investiture fall in the 740s, not the 710s. Shifting the segment by
the computed error gives the modern chronology: Durlabhavardhana
c. 625–661 (which also makes him, correctly, the king on the throne
when Xuanzang arrived in 631 — Kalhaṇa's unadjusted figures put the
wrong man there), **Candrapīḍa c. 712/13–720, Lalitāditya
c. 724/725–760/761**.

**Verdict: CORRECTED.** This is the campaign's biggest new
correction: the accepted dates of the Kārkoṭa kings — including
Lalitāditya, the most famous king of early-medieval Kashmir — are
the Chinese-corrected dates, and the correction is a generation
(~25–36 years), not a rounding. **Firmness: Firm** for the annalistic
events (embassy, investiture, edicts are court record); **Probable**
for the name equations on which the transfer to Kalhaṇa's kings
depends. **Strongest objection:** the correction is *local*. The
annals touch only the segment they transact with (631–747); they
cannot repair the *Rājataraṅgiṇī*'s deep chronology, where Kalhaṇa's
inflation runs to centuries, and even within the Kārkoṭa segment the
+31-year shift assumes Kalhaṇa's *relative* regnal years are sound
and only his epoch is mis-set — if the regnal years themselves are
corrupt, the annals give two fixed kings and a floating line between
them.

## Case 10 — Nepal: Narendradeva's restoration

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** Nepal's Licchavi-period
chronology is the campaign's worst Indian-side case. The later
Nepalese vaṃśāvalīs give king lists with legendary reign lengths and
no era discipline; the Licchavi inscriptions are dated in an era
whose identity was itself fought over for a century (the Lévi-era
debate), so even epigraphic dates floated by conversion; and the
mid-7th-century sequence — Aṃśuvarman's death, the
Jiṣṇugupta/Dhruvadeva tangle, the usurpation, Narendradeva's exile
and return — could be arranged across the century at a scholar's
discretion. Narendradeva in particular had no absolute date from any
Nepalese source.

**The Chinese record.** The *Jiu Tang shu* (Old Book of Tang), Nepal
(Nipoluo) account — a gazetteer compiled from the reports of the
640s — states the sequence plainly: on the death of Narendradeva's
father, his uncle **Viṣṇugupta** (Yu.sna kug.ti) seized the throne;
Narendradeva fled to Tibet; **"the Tibetans gave him refuge and
re-established him on his throne"** — the restoration is pegged to
**641** by the Tibetan chronological frame (Songtsen Gampo's Nepal
intervention, the year of the Wencheng marriage; the Old Tibetan
Annals place the Nepal campaign in the early 640s). The same Tang
record complex dates his floruit from the other side: Nepal supplied
**7,000 troops** to Wang Xuance's 648 campaign against Harṣa's
usurper (Case 8), and the Tangshu's description of Nepal's king —
pearls, rock-crystal, the Buddha-image amulet — describes
Narendradeva as the reigning king in information current to c. 648.

**The derivation.** Restoration **641** (Tang/Tibetan peg); reigning
in **647–648** (the troop contribution and the gazetteer's present
tense). The late-Licchavi sequence thereby gains its only absolute
peg in this segment: Aṃśuvarman's line ends, the usurpation and the
exile fall, and Narendradeva's accession locks to 641 — against
which the inscriptional era readings and the vaṃśāvalī lists must
now be arranged, instead of the other way round.

**Verdict: CORRECTED.** An event at the centre of Nepalese history —
the restoration that made Nepal a Tibetan client for a generation —
has its date solely from the Tang/Tibetan record; the vaṃśāvalī
chronology is not adjusted by the correction so much as replaced by
it for this segment. **Firmness: Probable** (the event sequence is
annalistic and the Tibetan annals concur; the exact year 641 is the
standard scholarly peg within the 641–643 window rather than a year
stated in so many words in the Tangshu text). **Strongest
objection:** the Tangshu narrative is compressed to the point of
distortion — it makes the usurper a single uncle, Viṣṇugupta, where
the inscriptions show a Jiṣṇugupta/Dhruvadeva co-rule tangle — so
the record that dates the restoration also garbles its dramatis
personae; a sceptic can accept the peg while doubting that the man
restored in 641 maps cleanly onto the inscriptional Narendradeva
whose dated records, on some era readings, run implausibly late.

## Case 11 — The Hūṇas: Song Yun in Gandhāra, Xuanzang on Mihirakula

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** The Hūṇa frame is one of
Indian chronology's better-built ones, because it is epigraphic:
Toramāṇa's inscriptions; his defeat by Prakāśadharman in the Risthal
inscription (dated 515); Mihirakula's Gwalior inscription (his
regnal year 15); Yaśodharman's Mandsaur inscriptions (Mālava year
589 = **532 CE**) claiming victory over Mihirakula. Modern placement:
**Mihirakula c. 515–542**, defeated c. 525–532. What the Indian side
lacks is any contemporary narrative witness.

**The Chinese record.** Two witnesses of very different quality.
(i) **Song Yun**, in Gandhāra in **520** (embassy 518–522, spine
C14): the country is ruled by a Hūṇa king — cruel, unbelieving,
permanently on campaign with his army — whose people conquered
Gandhāra **two generations** before. He also describes the Kaniṣka
stūpa still standing. Song Yun does not name the king; the date,
theatre, and character have made the identification with Mihirakula
standard. (ii) **Xuanzang** (c. 630) tells the full Mihirakula
story — the insulted king turned persecutor, the defeat and capture
by the Gupta king Bālāditya, the release, the Kashmiri epilogue —
but places him **"several hundred years"** before his own time.

**The derivation.** Song Yun's 520 eyewitness falls inside the
epigraphic floruit (515–542) and his "two generations since the
conquest" puts the Hūṇa takeover of Gandhāra at c. 460–490,
consistent with the Indian-side c. 465–475. Xuanzang's narrative
corroborates the Bālāditya-defeat tradition independently of
Yaśodharman's self-interested inscription — two hostile-to-Hūṇa
witness traditions (Gupta court memory via Xuanzang's informants;
Aulikara epigraphy) triangulating a defeat c. 525–535. But
Xuanzang's interval is simply **wrong**: Mihirakula ended c. 542,
Xuanzang travelled 629–645 — a gap of ~100 years, not "several
hundred." The campaign logs this as its clearest Chinese-side
interval error: the spine's pilgrims are exact about what they see
and careless about elapsed centuries, which is exactly the
asymmetry the method assumes (observation: lag ≈ 0 and reliable;
reported intervals: tradition, graded as such).

**Verdict: CORROBORATED** — the Chinese record confirms the
epigraphic Hūṇa frame at its one narrative point (a Hūṇa king ruling
Gandhāra in 520) and independently transmits the defeat tradition,
while contributing one flagged error of its own. **Firmness:
Probable** (Song Yun's king is unnamed; the Mihirakula
identification, though standard, is an inference). **Strongest
objection:** if Song Yun's king is not Mihirakula but a Hephthalite
overlord west of the Indus (Cosmas, c. 535, describes a White Hun
king with 2,000 elephants in that quarter), the corroboration
attaches to the wrong man and the case reduces to "a Hūṇa ruled
Gandhāra in 520" — which the Indian evidence already knew.

## Case 12 — The Gupta frame: Faxian under Candragupta II *(corroboration test)*

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** The Gupta chronology is
the firmest in early India: the Gupta era (epoch 319/320 CE) is
epigraphically continuous, and Candragupta II's reign (c. 375–415)
is fixed by dated inscriptions (his own records run to Gupta year
93 = 412 CE) and coinage. The pre-registered test: does the one
contemporary foreign witness inside the empire confirm the frame,
or disturb it?

**The Chinese record.** Faxian's *Foguo ji* (spine C11): he left
Chang'an in **399**, spent three years at Pāṭaliputra copying
Vinaya, two at Tāmralipti, and returned by sea, reaching China
412–414. His Madhyadeśa is a prosperous, lightly governed kingdom —
no capital punishment, no household registration, charitable
dispensaries in the capital, a flourishing monastic establishment —
ruled from Pāṭaliputra by a king **he never names**.

**The derivation.** Faxian's Indian years (c. 401–411) fall squarely
inside Candragupta II's inscriptionally dated reign, at its
documented centre (Pāṭaliputra), in the conditions (paramount,
stable, wealthy) the Gupta frame predicts. Nothing in his account
requires or suggests any adjustment: no rival king, no disturbed
succession, no chronological friction.

**Verdict: CORROBORATED.** The frame holds under an independent
contemporary witness — which is what a corroboration test is for,
and the campaign counts it a pass, not a null. **Firmness:
Probable**, for one registered reason: the king is unnamed, so the
synchronism identifies the *frame* (a paramount Gupta-style kingdom
at Pāṭaliputra in 399–414), and the equation of that frame with
Candragupta II personally is an inference from the Indian side's
own dates — the corroboration is of the chronology's consistency,
not an independent measurement of the reign. **Strongest objection:**
precisely that circularity — Faxian corroborates the Gupta frame
only because we already know whose frame it is; had the inscriptional
dates been wrong by a generation, Faxian's anonymous prosperity
would have "corroborated" the wrong king just as smoothly. The
campaign accepts the verdict at Probable and no higher for exactly
this reason.


## Case 13 — Chola regnal chronology vs the Song annals

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** Imperial Chola chronology
is inscriptionally dense — thousands of records dated in regnal
years, anchored by astronomical details and the era conversions of
the praśastis — and its accepted frame (Rājarāja I 985–1014,
Rājendra I co-regent 1012, sole 1014–1044, Kulottunga I 1070–1120)
is among the most secure in medieval India. Like Case 12, this is a
test of whether the foreign record confirms or catches it.

**The Chinese record.** The Song annals' Zhulian (Chola) file
records three embassies, each naming the sending king. **1015
(Dazhong Xiangfu 8):** the first Chola mission reaches the Song
court; the king is named **Lo-ts'a-lo-ts'a = Rājarāja**. **1033
(Mingdao 2):** a mission from **Shi-lo-cha Yin-to-lo Chu-lo = Śrī
Rāja Indra Chola = Rājendra**. **1077 (Xining 10):** a mission of
seventy merchants from **Ti-hua-kia-lo = Deva Kulottunga =
Kulottunga I**, received with 81,800 strings of copper cash for its
tribute goods. The same Song file preserves a bonus datum: before
1015, Śrīvijaya (Sanfoqi) had represented Chola at the Song court
as its *dependency* — the 1015 mission is Chola correcting the
record in person, and a later Song memorial admits the court's
protocol error (coarse paper, plain envelope) that the
misrepresentation caused.

**The derivation.** Check each name against the inscriptional
frame. 1015: Rājarāja died in 1014 — the mission, dispatched from
India one to two sailing seasons earlier, was sent in his name and
arrived months after his death; the Chinese record's "king
Rājarāja, 1015" is the dispatch-side truth and is consistent with
the Indian dates to within the voyage lag. 1033: Rājendra sole
ruler 1014–1044 — exact. 1077: Kulottunga I acceded 1070 — the
annals independently confirm him on the throne seven years into
the inscriptionally computed reign. The Kaḍāram expedition
(1025, known from Rājendra's Tamil stele) leaves an indirect
Chinese trace — Sanfoqi's missions and standing at the Song court
resume and shift after 1025 — but the Song annals do not date the
raid itself, and the campaign does not claim they do.

**Verdict: CORROBORATED** — name-for-name, at three points across
sixty years, the strongest corroboration in the campaign. **Firmness:
Firm** (annalistic entries with reign-era dates; the name equations
are phonetically transparent). **Strongest objection:** corroboration
of a chronology this inscriptionally dense adds little information —
the Chola frame was never in doubt, so the case's real yield is
methodological (the spine works on the medieval material exactly as
on the ancient) plus the one thing the Indian record could not
supply: dated proof of how Chola presented itself abroad, and
Śrīvijaya's prior misrepresentation of it, in 1015.

## Case 14 — Śrīvijaya's rise via Yijing

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** (Southeast Asian, but the
campaign's pre-registration includes it: Śrīvijaya is the
transmission channel's other shore.) Śrīvijaya has no indigenous
chronicle tradition at all; its chronology is built from its own Old
Malay inscriptions, the earliest of which — Kedukan Bukit — is dated
604 Śaka = **682 CE**, with Kota Kapur (686) and Talang Tuwo (684)
in the same decade. On epigraphy alone, the kingdom appears in 682
fully armed, and its "rise" is undatable guesswork placed anywhere
in the 7th century.

**The Chinese record.** Yijing stopped at Fo-shih (Śrīvijaya) in
**671** for six months on the way to India, again in **685** for a
longer stay (to 689) on the way back. His testimony: in 671 Fo-shih
is a fortified royal city with **more than a thousand monks**,
whose learning he ranks with Madhyadeśa's, where he himself studied
Sanskrit grammar (Śabdavidyā) as preparation for India and where he
recommends Chinese students do the same; the king patronises the
establishment. And a political datum with a date on both ends: in
**673** he passed Mo-lo-yu (Malayu, up the Batang Hari) as a
separate country; by his return stay (**685–689**) "Mo-lo-yu has
become Fo-shih" — Malayu absorbed into Śrīvijaya.

**The derivation.** A kingdom with a royal capital, a thousand-monk
learning establishment, and an international reputation sufficient
to draw a Chinese pilgrim's six-month study stay existed in
**671 — eleven years before its first surviving inscription**, and
institutions of that scale are not founded the year before they are
observed: the rise must be pushed back into the **660s at the
latest**, a decade-plus of pre-epigraphic maturity that the
inscriptional record alone could never have shown. The Malayu
annexation — a datable act of expansion — is bracketed to
**(673, 689]** by the two observations.

**Verdict: BOUNDED.** Yijing supplies the terminus the epigraphy
could not: Śrīvijaya's rise is bounded above by 671 (mature by
then), and its first datable expansion is bracketed to 673–689.
**Firmness: Firm** (dated eyewitness observation, the spine's
zero-lag class). **Strongest objection:** "mature in 671" bounds
the rise only by an unmeasurable margin — a entrepôt can grow a
monastic establishment in a decade, so the observation is
consistent with a founding as late as the early 660s and says
nothing about the 7th century before that; and the Fo-shih =
Palembang identification, though standard, is an identification —
if Fo-shih's centre sat elsewhere on the coast, the bound attaches
to the polity but not to the site the inscriptions name.

## Case 15 — Śaṅkara *(the expected NO BITE control)*

**The Indian-side estimate as it stands.** Śaṅkara's date is a real
Indian guesswork problem: the maṭha traditions give 788–820 CE
(and wilder figures besides); modern scholarship, from his relation
to Kumārila and Maṇḍana and from Kamalaśīla's Tattvasaṅgraha
(c. 750s) already engaging Advaita positions, converges loosely on
**c. 700–750 CE**. A two-generation uncertainty, resting entirely on
relative-order and doxographic arguments.

**The Chinese record.** There is none, and the campaign checked the
places it would have to be. No Tang catalogue (through the Kaiyuan
catalogue of 730 and its successors) translates or lists any work of
Śaṅkara or any Vedānta text. Xuanzang (d. 664) describes non-Buddhist
schools, Vedānta-like doctrines among them, without naming him or
any contemporary Vedāntin. Yijing (d. 713) — who names grammarians,
logicians, and monks by the dozen, and who is this campaign's best
witness for exactly Śaṅkara's century — never mentions him. Chinese
translation of Upaniṣadic/Vedāntic material belongs to many
centuries later.

**Why the silence cannot be used.** An argument from this silence
("not famous by 713, therefore later") would require the China
channel to have been capable of registering a Brahmanical
śākhā-internal teacher. The spine's central structural finding
(CHINESE-SPINE §5, link 1) is that it was not: the channel carried
Buddhist texts and Buddhist-translated system material only, and
**no** Brahmanical text of any date — early or late — appears in it.
Śaṅkara's absence is therefore predicted with equal force on every
candidate date from 600 to 900 CE, and a datum predicted by all
hypotheses discriminates among none of them.

**Verdict: NO BITE** — as pre-registered, and the campaign reports
it without disappointment: the control's function is to prove the
grading can say no. Śaṅkara's date remains where Indian evidence
left it (c. 700–750, contested), untouched by this method.
**Strongest objection to the NO BITE itself:** one might argue the
silence is not *perfectly* uninformative — Yijing does register
Brahmanical learning (grammar above all) and names Bhartṛhari, a
Brahmanical author, so the channel was not hermetically Buddhist;
a sufficiently famous all-India Vedāntin might have leaked into it.
The campaign's answer: Bhartṛhari leaked in *as a grammarian*,
through the one Brahmanical discipline the Buddhist curriculum
itself consumed (vyākaraṇa); Vedānta as a school tradition had no
such carrier, and a single ambiguous silence cannot be converted
into a bound without exactly the overreach this campaign exists to
refuse.

## Campaign balance sheet

- **The method's centre of gravity is exactly where the
  pre-registration put it:** dated court and catalogue paper
  (embassies, investitures, colophons) corrects and corroborates;
  pilgrim observation corroborates and bounds; reported *intervals*
  inside pilgrim and biographical texts (Xuanzang's "several hundred
  years," the *Life of Vasubandhu*'s nirvāṇa arithmetic, the dotted
  record's lineage count) are tradition wearing the record's clothes,
  and are graded as tradition wherever they appear — including when
  they arrive in Chinese.
- **The single most transferable finding is negative-positive:**
  Chinese evidence is an *independent* witness only when the event
  it records happened on Chinese soil or at a Chinese desk (an
  embassy received, a translation completed, a pilgrim present).
  The moment the Chinese text transmits an Indian institution's own
  numbers (Case 1), it inherits that institution's errors and its
  agreement with Indian tradition proves nothing.

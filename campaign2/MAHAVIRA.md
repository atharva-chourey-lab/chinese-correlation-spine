# Campaign 2, Dedicated Build E — Mahāvīra and the śramaṇa world

Run 2026-11-17 against `campaign2/CAMPAIGN2-PREREG.md`, Addendum E
(registered and committed before grading, on Campaign 1's Case-16
pattern).

**The structural fact that governs the whole build.** The China
channel carried Buddhism. Jainism sent China no embassy, received
no Chinese pilgrim as a guest of its own institutions, and was
never translated by any bureau. So this build's Chinese evidence
arrives *entirely through Buddhist custody* — Buddhist texts that
mention the rivals, and Buddhist pilgrims who walked past their
communities. That custody is itself graded throughout: a rival's
doctrine as summarised by his opponents is evidence for the
rival's existence and fame, and for almost nothing else without a
discount.

---

## E1 — Mahāvīra himself: a date chained to the Buddha's

**The traditional dates, and what they are made of.** Both Jain
sects agree on the birth, **599 BCE**, and differ on the death:
**527 BCE** (Śvetāmbara) or **510 BCE** (a Digambara reckoning).
The 527 figure is not a transmitted memory; it is era arithmetic,
and the campaign can show the joins. The Śvetāmbara construction:
Mahāvīra died **470 years before the Vikrama era** — 57 + 470 =
**527**. The Digambara construction: he died **605 years before
the Śaka era** — 605 − 78 = **527** again. Two different eras, two
different intervals, one identical answer: the intervals were
chosen to fit the answer, which is what a construction looks like
when you catch it from two sides. And Build C of this campaign
has already dissolved the anchor of the first construction: the
Vikrama era bore the names Kṛta and Mālava for its first nine
centuries and was attached to a "Vikramāditya" only in the
9th–10th century CE. The Vīra Nirvāṇa Saṃvat is a medieval
calendar hung on a 5th-century BCE man.

**The scholarly brackets.** The oldest scholarly line runs through
the Jain tradition's own alternative synchronism: Hemacandra's
*Pariśiṣṭaparvan* places Mahāvīra's death **155 years before
Candragupta's accession** (c. 321 BCE) → **c. 476 BCE**; the
Hemacandra-based computations give birth 549, death **477 or
c. 468–467 BCE** — the figure Jacobi considered most plausible.
Modern scholarship, having moved the Buddha down (Build A), moves
Mahāvīra with him: "as much as 100 years early" is the standard
caution on 527.

**The synchronism, and who died first — per source class.** This
is the one place the two traditions genuinely interlock, and they
do not interlock to the same answer:

- **The Pali canon (Buddhist custody):** the Buddha is still
  preaching when news arrives that Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta has died at
  Pāvā and his community has split over the succession (the
  setting of the Pāsādika and Saṅgīti suttas). On this witness,
  **Mahāvīra died first**, in the Buddha's last years.
- **The Jain synchronism (Jain custody):** Hemacandra's 155-years-
  before-Candragupta gives 476 BCE — which, run against the
  *long* Buddha chronology (parinirvāṇa 483), puts Mahāvīra's
  death **after** the Buddha's. Run against the Bechert Buddha
  (c. 400), the same 476 puts him three generations *before*.
  The Jain synchronism does not fix the order; it inherits
  whichever Buddha it is run against.
- **Both traditions agree** on the frame that matters: the two men
  were contemporaries in the same small world — both know
  Bimbisāra and Ajātaśatru (whom the Jain texts claim as a Jain
  devotee and the Buddhist texts as a Buddhist patron — each
  tradition annexing the same king), both operate in Magadha and
  the Vajjian confederacy, and the Buddhist canon treats
  Nātaputta as the head of an already-established order, i.e. the
  senior man.

**The chained interval (this build's only number).** Build A
graded the parinirvāṇa to **[c. 500, c. 350] BCE** on total
evidence, with the Bechert centre at c. 400 ± 30. Chain Mahāvīra
to it through the Pali predecease — the strongest of the
synchronism witnesses, because it is an *opponent's* admission
(the Buddhist canon gains nothing by aging its rival) — and the
sharpest defensible statement is: **Mahāvīra died before the
Buddha, within the Buddha's last decade or so, putting his death
in [c. 500, c. 360] BCE across the full Buddha interval, and in
the late 5th century (c. 430–400) on the Bechert centre**; his
birth, on the tradition's own 72-year lifespan, a lifetime
earlier. The traditional 527 sits just outside even the chained
interval's early edge — reachable only if the long Buddha
chronology (486) *and* a ~40-year predecease both hold, which is
two favours, not one.

**The Chinese record's contribution to E1: none, directly.**
No Chinese source names Mahāvīra as a historical person with a
date; he enters Chinese only as Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta inside
translated Buddhist texts (E2), where he is a doctrinal opponent,
not a dated king. **Verdict E1: BOUNDED** — bounded entirely by
the Indian interlock and Build A's chain; the bound is real
(it excludes 599–527 as a *measured* placement and grades it a
construction) but no Chinese record tightens it. **Firmness:
Probable** (the predecease is a single-tradition witness, albeit
a hostile one; the era-arithmetic critique is Firm).
**Strongest objection:** the predecease rests on Buddhist texts
whose own stratification is debated — if the Pāsādika frame is
late, the chain's link is a tradition about a tradition, and
Mahāvīra's interval widens back toward the Jain figures. The
campaign accepts the widening: the verdict is a bound, and bounds
are allowed to be only as strong as their weakest link, stated.

## E2 — The six teachers in Chinese translation: a fossilised list, dated

**What exactly is attested.** The six rival teachers of the
Buddha's age — Pūraṇa Kassapa, Makkhali Gosāla, Ajita Kesakambala,
Pakudha Kaccāyana, Sañjaya Belaṭṭhiputta and Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta —
survive as a *fixed set with fixed doctrines* in the
Śrāmaṇyaphala-sūtra (the discourse on the fruits of the ascetic
life, framed as Ajātaśatru's confession). The Chinese transmission
carries it in dated translations: the *Dīrghāgama* (T.1), read out
by Buddhayaśas and translated by Zhu Fonian at Chang'an in
**412–413 CE**, whose sūtra 27 (the *Shamenguo jing*) is the
Śrāmaṇyaphala with the six teachers' doctrines in order; separate
earlier Chinese versions of the same sūtra exist (T.22 among
them); and Yijing's translation of the Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinaya's
Saṅghabhedavastu (**710 CE**, T.1450) carries the second great
recension of the same material. The Brahmajāla (Dīrghāgama sūtra
21, the *Fandong jing*) adds the 62-view catalogue — the śramaṇa
world's doctrinal census, Nigaṇṭha positions among them.

**What the dates do — the lag discipline applied honestly.** A
413 CE translation, run through the spine's lag distribution,
does *not* yield the teachers' floruit: these are not texts the
teachers wrote, they are a rival's catalogue of them, already
formulaic. What the TAQ genuinely bounds is the **stratum**: by
413 CE at the latest — and, since a translation presupposes a
settled recension, in reality by the early centuries CE — the
six-teachers list existed in fixed, translatable form, with each
man's doctrine reduced to its one-line caricature (Pūraṇa's
no-action, Gosāla's fatalism, Ajita's annihilationism, Pakudha's
seven uncreated substances, Sañjaya's eel-wriggling, Nātaputta's
fourfold restraint). A list that stable is a fossil: the men had
been dead long enough to become a syllabus. That is a real,
if modest, bound — it kills any hypothesis on which the six are
late inventions of the Buddhist schools (inventions do not
fossilise identically across the Dharmaguptaka and
Mūlasarvāstivāda recensions carried by different translation
teams three centuries apart), while saying nothing about whether
any individual doctrine-summary is fair. Gosāla's fatalism as the
Buddhist texts give it is the Ājīvika *niyati* doctrine through
hostile glass; the campaign grades the *existence and fixity* of
the list, not the fairness of its entries.

**Verdict E2: BOUNDED.** The six-teachers stratum is bounded above
by 413 CE (translation, Firm) and characterised as fossilised by
then; the teachers themselves are dated only by the E1 chain
(they are the Buddha's contemporaries in every recension, which
is a synchronism of tradition class). **Firmness: Firm** for the
TAQ; the fossilisation inference, Probable. **New anchor:**
`six-teachers-dirghagama-413`.

## E3 — The pilgrims' snapshot: the śramaṇa world, still standing in 638

**The record.** Xuanzang's India taxonomy (Xiyu ji, juan 2)
describes the Nirgranthas (Li-hi) from observation: naked,
hair-plucking ascetics, "their skin all cracked, their feet hard
and chapped, like rotting trees near rivers." On the ground in
Bengal (c. 638) he counts them twice as the dominant non-Buddhist
presence: at **Puṇḍravardhana**, among some hundred deva temples,
"the naked Nirgranthas are the most numerous"; at **Samataṭa**,
the same sentence. Vaiśālī — Mahāvīra's own country — shows the
same sectary landscape. Yijing, a generation later, includes the
Nirgranthas in his survey of India's schools as a going concern.

**What it pins.** A dated distribution map, nothing more — and
the campaign grades it for exactly that: **Jain communities were
numerous and publicly visible across northern and eastern India
in 629–695 CE**, eleven centuries after their founder on any
chronology. Survival at that scale is a datum Indian sources of
the 7th century (overwhelmingly courtly and Brahmanical or
Buddhist) rarely state so plainly, and it corroborates the
epigraphic picture (Jain dedications continue through the Gupta
and post-Gupta centuries) from an outside observer with no stake
in it. What it does not pin: origins, dates, doctrine, and —
notably — the Digambara/Śvetāmbara split, which the pilgrims'
naked/clothed observations hint at regionally but never name as
a schism.

**Verdict E3: CORROBORATED** (the survival and distribution of
the Nirgrantha order in the 7th century, eyewitness class).
**Firmness: Probable** (the identifications Ni-kien = Nirgrantha
= Jain are standard; the pilgrims' headcounts are impressions,
not censuses). **New anchor:** `nirgranthas-xuanzang-bengal-c638`.

## E4 — Pārśva: pure chain arithmetic, graded as such

**The claim.** Pārśva, 23rd tīrthaṅkara, died **250 years before
Mahāvīra** — the Jain canon states the interval and notes that
Mahāvīra's own parents were followers of Pārśva's order, which is
the tradition's internal reason for believing in the link.
Traditional arithmetic: 527 + 250 = **777 BCE**. Chained
arithmetic on E1's interval: death somewhere in the 8th–7th
century BCE. Scholarly opinion generally accepts Pārśva as
historical — the fourfold restraint attributed to him (against
Mahāvīra's five vows) is exactly the kind of unflattering
archaism traditions do not invent — but his *date* is the
interval, and the interval is a round number in a canon.

**The Chinese record.** Nothing. No Chinese text of any class
names Pārśva; the Buddhist sources that preserve his successor's
community (E2) never reach back to him; the pilgrims met his
order, not his memory. There is no second chain to check the
first against.

**Verdict E4: NO BITE** — and the case is reported as the
campaign's purest specimen of chain arithmetic: a figure whose
entire chronology is one round interval hung on one contested
date (E1), yielding a traditional year (777 BCE) with no more
evidentiary content than the 250 and the 527 that compose it.
If E1 moves, Pārśva moves; nothing else can move him.

## E5 — Makkhali Gosāla and the Ājīvikas

**The Indian side.** Gosāla is the best-attested of the six
teachers after Nātaputta: the Buddhist and Jain canons both
preserve long, hostile, mutually independent accounts of him
(the Jain *Bhagavatī Sūtra*'s Gosāla cycle is a biography
written by enemies who knew the milieu), and the Ājīvikas he is
said to have led received caves from Aśoka himself — the
Barābar cave inscriptions dedicate shelters to the Ājīvikas in
Aśoka's regnal years 12 and 19, the hardest Indian-side evidence
in this whole build, and entirely Indian.

**The Chinese side.** Gosāla enters Chinese only inside the
six-teachers list (E2) — a name and a one-line doctrine
(fatalism), in Buddhist custody, with the 413 TAQ on the list as
a whole. Of the Ājīvika *order* as a living body, the pilgrims
say nothing identifiable: Xuanzang's heretic taxonomies name
Nirgranthas, ash-smeared Śaivas and others, but no unambiguous
Ājīvika community — a silence the campaign prices as weak
(the pilgrims' categories are coarse, and the Ājīvikas' known
later survival is in the Tamil south, at the edge of Xuanzang's
itinerary), while noting it points the same way as the Indian
evidence for the order's northern decline by the 7th century.

**Verdict E5: NO BITE** for Gosāla the man and for the order —
his floruit is the Buddha-synchronism of tradition class (E2's
list), his sect's Aśokan floruit is epigraphic and Indian, and
China adds the list's TAQ and nothing else.

## E6 — Ālāra Kālāma and Uddaka Rāmaputta: the expected zero, registered and graded

**The claim.** The Buddha's two teachers — Ālāra, who taught him
the sphere of nothingness, and Uddaka, who taught the sphere of
neither-perception-nor-non-perception — are the first two names
in every Buddha biography, and the first two men the Buddha
wished to teach after the enlightenment, learning both had just
died. If any śramaṇa figures besides the six were going to carry
a date, it would be these, by their position in the story.

**The Chinese record.** They exist in Chinese exactly where the
preregistration predicted: inside translated Buddha-biographies —
the *Buddhacarita* (T.192, trans. 412–421), the *Lalitavistara*
(T.186, trans. 308), the *Abhiniṣkramaṇa-sūtra* (T.190, trans.
587–591) — as episodes in the Bodhisattva's education. The
translation dates bound the *biographical stratum* (the
two-teachers episode was fixed biography by the early 5th
century at latest), which no one doubted. No annal, no pilgrim,
no catalogue treats either man as a person in the world; neither
has a school, a text, or a follower in any Chinese source; and
unlike the six teachers they do not even fossilise into a list —
they fossilise into a *scene*.

**Verdict E6: NO BITE**, as registered. Their dates are the
Buddha's dates minus a few years, by narrative construction, and
the campaign reports the zero because a method that skips its
expected zeros is hiding its denominator.

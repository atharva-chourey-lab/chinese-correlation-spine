## D1 — Zhang Qian's Shendu report: the founding record dates itself

**The record.** Zhang Qian left Chang'an in 138 BCE, spent a
decade in Xiongnu captivity, reached Dayuan, Kangju, the Yuezhi
and Daxia (Bactria), and returned in **126 BCE**; Sima Qian
preserved his report in the *Shiji* (ch. 123). In a Bactrian
market he saw **cloth from Shu and bamboo staffs from Qiong**
(Sichuan goods) and was told the merchants bought them in
**Shendu** — India — several thousand *li* southeast of Daxia,
whose people ride elephants to war and live beside a great river.
He reasoned a southwest route from Shu must exist; the emperor's
envoys sent to find it were blocked and got no further than the
Dian country (Yunnan).

**The derivation.** Two facts, of different classes. (i) *Firm:*
by c. 128 BCE, Sichuan goods were already reaching Bactria
through India — the trade predates the documentation, and the
report is China's founding dated notice of India. (ii) *Structur
...[truncated 8536 chars]al:* the
report is explicitly second-hand for India — Zhang Qian never
entered Shendu — and it names no Indian king, city or event.
What it dates is the *contact*: China's knowledge of India, and
the trade's existence, both fixed at the report's own date.

**Verdict D1: BOUNDED.** The spine's earliest record bounds the
contact horizon (India known, and trading with Sichuan, by
c. 128 BCE) and bounds nothing in Indian chronology — the
zero-king content of the founding page sets the pattern the whole
spine obeys: China dates the events of its own archive.
**Firmness: Firm** (the mission's frame is annalistic history;
the India content graded as the hearsay it declares itself to be).

## D2 — Aśoka in Chinese records: a legend with a TAQ, a king without one

**The record.** Aśoka enters Chinese in force as legend: the
*Ayuwang zhuan* (T.2042, trans. An Faqin, fl. 281–306; re-translated
512) carries the full cycle — the gift of dirt, the 84,000 stūpas,
Upagupta — and Build A graded its stratum. The pilgrims add site
lore: Xuanzang reports Aśoka stūpas, pillars and hells across
India, every one of them received attribution, none of them
observation of anything Mauryan. The annals, which name Indian
kings when embassies occur, know no Aśoka embassy and no Aśoka:
the Mauryan court and the Chinese court never transacted, two
centuries before Zhang Qian (D1) opened the file.

**Verdict D2: BOUNDED.** The legend stratum is bounded (formed by
c. 306, A1); the historical Aśoka is dated by his own edicts and
the Greek synchronism (c. 268 consecration — the spine's sheet
anchor, non-Chinese), and the Chinese record's role is confined
to preserving, in translation, the interval traditions graded in
Build A. **Firmness: Firm** for the TAQ; the negative on the
annals is a checked negative across the *Hou Hanshu* horizon.

## D3 — The succession crisis of 647–648: the campaign's clean CORRECTED

**The Indian-side estimate.** There is none. No Indian source —
not Bāṇa, not any inscription, not any later chronicle — records
what happened when Harṣa died: no death date (Campaign 1 Case 8),
and no succession. The throne of Kannauj simply goes dark in
Indian evidence in the 640s.

**The Chinese record** (graded in Campaign 1 for the death date;
graded *here* for the distinct event of the succession). Wang
Xuance's second mission, dispatched **647**, reached Magadha to
find Harṣa dead and **Aruṇāśa** — a ministerial usurper, on the
standard reading — on the throne. The usurper attacked the
mission; Wang escaped to Tibet, borrowed **7,000 Nepalese horse**
and Tibetan infantry, returned in **648**, defeated Aruṇāśa in a
three-day battle, and presented him captive at Chang'an, where
the court staged the submission. The Tang annals carry the
sequence with reign-era dates at every step.

**Verdict D3: CORRECTED** — in Campaign 1 Case 8's strongest
sense: the Chinese record does not adjust an Indian fact, it
*supplies* one Indian history does not contain — who took the
throne, what he did, and how he ended, dated to the year.
**Firmness: Firm** for the annalistic event sequence; **Probable**
for the usurper's identity (Aruṇāśa is the annals' transcription;
his equation with an Indian "Arjuna," and his exact office, are
scholarly reconstruction). **Strongest objection:** as in Case 8,
the chain is single-source at root (Wang's own lost report behind
the annals) — accepted there, accepted here, with the same
defence: internally dated, uncontradicted, and uncontradictable
from an Indian side that recorded nothing.

## D4 — Pulakeśin II: the pilgrim confirms the praśasti

**The Indian-side claim.** The Aihole praśasti (634 CE, Case C5's
anchor) boasts that Pulakeśin II defeated Harṣa — the paramount
power of the north — on the Narmadā. A victor's inscription is
interested evidence, and the defeat's date (c. 618–619) is a
scholarly placement from the inscriptional sequence.

**The Chinese record.** Xuanzang, in Maharashtra c. **641**,
describes the reigning king — enterprising, far-sighted, obeyed
by his vassals — and reports, as common knowledge of the country,
that Śīlāditya (Harṣa) had mustered the troops of the Five Indies
against him repeatedly and **could not subdue him**. The pilgrim
names no name (the identification with Pulakeśin II is from the
Indian frame), but the attestation is independent, contemporary,
and disinterested: a foreign monk with no Chalukya patron to
please, recording within a few years of Aihole that the northern
emperor's Deccan war had failed.

**Verdict D4: CORROBORATED.** The substance of the Aihole claim —
Harṣa checked by the Deccan king — is confirmed by an outside
witness; the defeat's *date* stays where Indian evidence put it.
**Firmness: Probable** (king unnamed in the pilgrim's text; the
frame identification is standard).

## D5 — Bhāskaravarman: one king, two independent Chinese directions

**The Indian-side estimate.** Kāmarūpa's king is known from his
own inscriptions (the Nidhanpur plates) and from Bāṇa — none of
it absolutely dated. His reign's placement floats on the Harṣa
synchronism alone.

**The Chinese record, twice.** (i) Xuanzang was invited to
Kāmarūpa, resided with Bhāskaravarman c. **642–643**, and was
escorted by him to Harṣa's Kannauj assembly — a dated eyewitness
floruit, the king *named* (contrast the Gupta anonymity of
Case C2). (ii) The Tang record of the 648 campaign has
Bhāskaravarman, after Aruṇāśa's defeat, supplying Wang Xuance's
force with cattle, horses and provisions — the record complex
gives 30,000 head of cattle — and preserves his celebrated
request for a portrait of Laozi and a translation of the
*Daodejing*: the king still reigning, and transacting with China,
in **648**.

**Verdict D5: CORROBORATED** — with a bounding effect the
campaign notes: the reign's late end, floating on the Indian
side, is fixed at 642–648 by two Chinese witnesses who did not
know each other's testimony. **Firmness: Probable** (the Laozi
request rides on the same record complex as the logistics and is
graded with it).

## D6 — The Pallavas: a named king in a dated court record

**The first half is a frame.** Xuanzang at Kāñcīpuram (c. 642)
describes Draviḍa country and its capital — the reigning Pallava
(Narasiṃhavarman I, on the Indian frame) unnamed, as pilgrims
usually leave kings. **The second half is a record.** In
**Kaiyuan 8 (720)**, eighth month, the Tang court received an
embassy from the king of South India whose name the record
transcribes *Che-li-Na-lo-seng-kia pao-to-pa-mo* — **Śrī
Narasiṃha Potavarman**, i.e. **Narasiṃhavarman II** (Rājasiṃha)
of Kanchi. The embassy's business is preserved: the king offers
his elephants and cavalry against the Arabs and the Tibetans,
and asks the emperor to name his army and a temple he has built
in the emperor's honour. Xuanzong names the army **"the army
which cherished virtue"** (Huai-de) and the temple **Guihua si**,
and sends a return embassy conferring a brevet title on the king.

**The derivation.** The inscriptional bracket for
Narasiṃhavarman II (c. 700–728) is here confirmed from outside,
by name, in a specific month of 720 — the same class of peg the
Song annals gave the Cholas (Campaign 1 Case 13), three centuries
earlier. Two cautions are part of the grade: the temple the
emperor named is *plausibly*, not provably, the Nagapattinam
vihāra later called the China Pagoda; and the oft-repeated claim
that the embassy was "led by Vajrabodhi" is Nilakanta Sastri's
inference — Vajrabodhi reached China in 720 by his own route, and
the annal does not name the envoy.

**Verdict D6: CORROBORATED. Firmness: Firm** (court record dated
to the month; name equation phonetically transparent and
standard).

## D7 — Samudragupta and the Meghavarṇa embassy: a join, graded as a join

**The textbook claim.** Meghavarṇa of Ceylon sent an embassy to
Samudragupta, obtained permission to build a monastery at Bodh
Gayā for Sinhalese pilgrims, and built the Mahābodhi Saṅghārāma.
Textbooks often credit the story to "the Chinese pilgrim
Xuanzang" (some to Wang Xuance). The campaign graded the join
link by link:

- **Xuanzang (c. 637)** describes the monastery standing north of
  the Bodhi tree — three storeys, six halls, over a thousand
  Sthavira monks — and transmits its foundation tradition: a king
  of Ceylon sent gifts to the Indian emperor and obtained the
  permission. **He names neither king.** His evidence bounds the
  monastery's existence (by 637) and corroborates the event-class.
- **The Allahabad praśasti** (Hariṣeṇa, for Samudragupta himself)
  lists the Siṃhalas among the island peoples who sent missions
  and gifts to Samudragupta. **It names no Ceylon king either.**
- **The named pairing** — Meghavarṇa ↔ Samudragupta — is therefore
  a scholarly join of two anonymous halves, and it carries a
  friction the textbooks absorb silently: Meghavarṇa's own dates
  are Ceylon-chronicle arithmetic, and his traditional bracket
  (early 4th century, often given c. 301–328) sits *before*
  Samudragupta's reign (c. 335–375) — the join requires moving the
  Ceylon king later, which the chronicle sums permit but do not
  compel.
- **The case's Chinese-side caution:** Yijing reports that a king
  **Śrīgupta** built a temple for Chinese pilgrims near
  Mṛgaśikhāvana "about 500 years" before his time — an interval
  routinely converted into a date for the Gupta founder (c. 175
  CE). It is graded here as Campaign 1 graded Xuanzang's "several
  hundred years" for Mihirakula (Case 11): a pilgrim's round
  interval is tradition, and a dynasty's founder cannot be dated
  from it.

**Verdict D7: BOUNDED.** The monastery is bounded (standing by
637, eyewitness); the embassy event-class is corroborated by two
independent anonymous witnesses; Samudragupta's dates are not
touched — his frame is the Gupta era's own, among the firmest in
early India, and needs no Chinese help. **Firmness: Probable.**

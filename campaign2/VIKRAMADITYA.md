# Campaign 2, Dedicated Build C — Vikramāditya: the phantom king

Run 2026-11-17 against `campaign2/CAMPAIGN2-PREREG.md` §C.

**The problem in one line.** "Vikramāditya" is a title — "Sun of
Valour" — borne by attested kings across five centuries, and
Indian legend fused them into one immortal king of Ujjain who
founded an era in 57 BCE, defeated the Śakas, and kept nine gems
(the navaratnas, Kālidāsa among them) at his court. It is the
Janaka problem at imperial scale: a title mistaken for a man, and
then a millennium of history hung on the man. The build grades
each bearer separately and asks of each the campaign's one
question: does the Chinese record reach him?

The bearers, before grading:

| Bearer | Dates | Title attested by |
|---|---|---|
| The legendary founder of Ujjain | epoch 58/57 BCE | legend only (see C1) |
| Candragupta II | r. c. 375–415 CE | his own coinage (Vikramāditya / Śrī Vikrama) |
| Skandagupta | r. c. 455–467 CE | his coinage (Vikramāditya / Kramāditya legends) |
| Yaśodharman of Mandasor | fl. 532 CE | association is later/scholarly (see C4) |
| The navaratna king | undatable | late legend texts (see C5) |

## C1 — The era epoch (58/57 BCE): a king who is not there

**The Indian-side dissolution (this part needs no China).** The
era of 57 BCE is real; its name is the fraud. Inscriptions of its
first nine centuries call it **Kṛta** (attested 343, 371 CE),
**Kṛtā** (404), or **"the era of the Mālava people"** (424) — never
Vikrama. The association with a king Vikramāditya appears in no
source before the **9th century**; the earliest inscription that
ties the era to a king of that name is dated **971 CE**, and the
earliest literary work making the connection is Amitagati's
*Subhāṣita-Ratna-Sandoha* (**993–994 CE**). The founder-king is
therefore a naming event of the 9th–10th century projected back a
thousand years. The older scholarly rescue — that the era is the
Azes era renamed — was discredited by Falk and Bennett, who place
the Azes epoch at 47/46 BCE, a decade off the Vikrama epoch.

**The Chinese record.** Zhang Qian's report (returned 126 BCE;
Case D1) describes Shendu at second hand and names no Indian king
anywhere. The *Hou Hanshu* Western Regions account — whose report
stream runs to c. 125 CE and which *does* record the Śaka-Yuezhi
world in dynastic detail (it is Campaign 1 Case 2's Kushan source)
— knows Tianzhu as a land of kingdoms and names no king of Malwa
or Ujjain, in the 1st century BCE or any other. No Chinese source
of any century names a 1st-century BCE Ujjain king.

**Verdict C1: NO BITE** — with a rider that makes this more than a
null: the Chinese silence here is *informed* silence. The Han
record stream demonstrably covered the era's own theatre (the
Śaka and Yuezhi movements are its subject), and a paramount king
who expelled the Śakas from Ujjain in 57 BCE is exactly the class
of event that stream registers when it happens a century later to
the Kushans. The phantom is dissolved by Indian epigraphy; the
Chinese record, checked, does not resurrect him. **Firmness:
Firm** (Indian side, epigraphic); the Chinese negative, Probable
(report streams are thin, and the campaign prices silence
accordingly — see Build B).

## C2 — Candragupta II Vikramāditya (r. c. 375–415)

**The Indian-side estimate.** The one fully historical
Vikramāditya: reign fixed by the Gupta era in his own dated
inscriptions (to Gupta year 93 = 412 CE), the title on his
coinage. Campaign 1 Case 12 already graded the Faxian frame
CORROBORATED and does not re-open.

**What is graded here is the naming claim** — the popular
statement that "Chinese records confirm Vikramāditya." They do
not, because they cannot: Faxian (in India 399–414, at the reign's
documented centre) **never names the king at all**. No Chinese
source of any date names Candragupta II, or any Gupta king of his
line. The Chinese channel names Indian kings precisely when a
court event gives it a name to record — Rājarāja in 1015,
Kulottunga in 1077, Narasiṃhavarman in 720 (Case D6) — and the
Gupta court sent it no embassy and received none that the annals
kept. What the Chinese record pins for this bearer is exhausted by
Campaign 1's verdict: a paramount, prosperous, Pāṭaliputra-centred
frame, 399–414, consistent with the reign and with nothing else
in particular.

**Verdict C2: BOUNDED.** The reign's frame is corroborated; the
man, his name and his title are unreachable from China, and every
claim that a Chinese source attests "Vikramāditya" (for *any*
bearer) is false. **Firmness: Probable**, inheriting Case 12's
registered circularity caution (the frame is identified as his by
Indian dates, not by the pilgrim).

## C3 — Skandagupta Vikramāditya and the Hūṇa wars

**The Indian-side frame.** Skandagupta (r. c. 455–467) repelled
the Hūṇas — his Bhitari pillar inscription claims the victory —
and his Junagadh records are dated Gupta years 136–138
(455–458 CE). His coinage bears the Vikramāditya/Kramāditya
titles. A generation later the Hūṇa war renews under Toramāṇa and
Mihirakula (Campaign 1 Case 11, king-side chronology: graded
there, not here).

**The Chinese record and the identification.** Xuanzang (c. 630)
tells the campaign's one relevant story: king **Bālāditya** of
Magadha defeated and captured Mihirakula, spared him at his
mother's intercession, and released him to his Kashmiri epilogue.
Who is Bālāditya? The standard identification is **Narasimhagupta
Bālāditya**, Skandagupta's nephew-generation successor — *not*
Skandagupta. Older literature occasionally blurred the Gupta
anti-Hūṇa kings into one figure; the epigraphy separates them,
and the campaign grades identifications, not blurs. Xuanzang
therefore attests: (a) an event-*tradition* — a Gupta king of
Magadha defeated, captured and released Mihirakula — received in
India a century after the fact, triangulating Yaśodharman's
self-interested Mandasor claim from an independent direction
(Case 11's corroboration, cited); (b) under a name that is not
Skandagupta's; (c) with the interval error ("several hundred
years") already flagged in Case 11 as this witness's known
failure mode. Of Skandagupta Vikramāditya himself — his name,
his title, his 455 Hūṇa victory — the Chinese record knows
nothing.

**Verdict C3: BOUNDED.** The defeat tradition is corroborated at
event level; the Bālāditya identification is graded **Probable**
(Narasimhagupta) and its equation with Skandagupta **rejected** on
the standard reading; the title-bearer himself is unreached.
**Strongest objection:** if Bālāditya were instead a throne-name
borne across the Gupta line, Xuanzang's story could carry
Skandagupta's war inside it; the campaign's answer is that an
identification that can absorb any Gupta king attests none.

## C4 — Yaśodharman (Mandasor, 532 CE)

**The Indian-side claim.** Yaśodharman's Mandasor inscriptions,
dated Mālava year 589 = **532 CE**, claim that Mihirakula — who
had bowed to no one — paid homage to him. He is "sometimes called
Vikramāditya" in later and popular writing, and Hoernlé once
proposed *him* as the king who renamed the era (see C1: the
renaming, whenever it happened, left its first trace in the
9th century, three centuries after him too).

**The Chinese record.** No Chinese source names Yaśodharman, any
king of Malwa in the 530s, or a victory over Mihirakula won from
Mandasor. Worse for the claim, the one Chinese-carried victor
tradition assigns the defeat to a *different* king in a different
kingdom (Bālāditya of Magadha, C3): the two Indian victor
traditions compete for one event, and the Chinese channel
transmits the rival. That is not corroboration of Yaśodharman; it
is a second claimant.

**Verdict C4: NO BITE.** Yaśodharman's date stands where his own
inscriptions put it (532 CE, era-dated, Indian-side and firm) —
untouched, unhelped, unharmed by this method.

## C5 — The legendary court, the navaratnas, and Kālidāsa

**The legend.** The nine gems — Kālidāsa at their head — belong to
the *Jyotirvidābharaṇa*, a pseudepigraphic work fathered on
Kālidāsa himself, and to the late story-collections built on it.
The same legend-complex made the grammarian Bhartṛhari the
legendary king's brother — the half of the legend Campaign 1
Case 7 already killed from the Chinese side (Yijing: Bhartṛhari
died c. 651–652 CE, seven centuries after his supposed brother's
era).

**Kālidāsa's real bracket (Indian-built, and it is enough).** The
hard TAQ is epigraphic: the **Aihole praśasti** of Pulakeśin II,
composed by Ravikīrti and dated Śaka 556 = **634 CE**, already
names Kālidāsa (with Bhāravi) as a standard of poetic fame —
a poet does not become a byword the year he writes, so Kālidāsa
stands classical *before* 634, and the scholarly working bracket
(4th–5th century, usually Gupta-age) sits comfortably inside the
TAQ. *(Registered correction to this campaign's own brief: the
Aihole TAQ was described to the campaign as "already in the
spine." It was not in `data/anchors.json`. It is added by this
build through the validated `add` path, flagged as a non-Chinese
external anchor on the Gondophares/Quilon precedent.)*

**The Chinese-translation evidence: none.** No work of Kālidāsa,
and no Sanskrit kāvya of any author, was translated into Chinese
in the premodern period; the Tang catalogues that list thousands
of Buddhist titles list no belles-lettres at all. The channel
carried śāstra and sūtra, not poetry (the same structural limit
that produced Build B's negative and Campaign 1's Śaṅkara
control). Kālidāsa's date is, and remains, an Indian epigraphic
and literary-historical construction — a good one, needing no
Chinese help and able to get none.

**Verdict C5: NO BITE** (China, on Kālidāsa and on the court) —
and the legend as a whole graded **DISSOLVED**, by combined
evidence: the era bore other names for nine centuries (C1), the
"brother" died in 651 (Campaign 1), and the court's star poet is
a classical byword by 634 at an Ujjain that had been Gupta,
Hūṇa-fought and Aulikara land in between — never the seat of one
immortal king.

**The flagship result of Campaign 2.** Of the five Vikramādityas,
the Chinese record pins **none by name** — and that negative,
assembled bearer by bearer, is the finding. The title's most
famous bearer is a 9th-century naming projected onto a 57 BCE
epoch; its most historical bearer (Candragupta II) is the one a
Chinese pilgrim lived under for a decade without ever learning —
or needing — his name. "Vikramāditya" survives grading exactly as
Janaka did in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka build: as a title that history
kept re-issuing, and that legend kept cashing as a man.

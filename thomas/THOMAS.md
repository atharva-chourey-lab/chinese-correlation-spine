# THE THOMAS FILE

**Thomas, the Gospel of Thomas, India, and the Chinese dating connection**

Built for the Chinese Correlation Spine (`~/workspace/chinese-correlation-spine/`).
Method, as registered for the spine: foreign dated records as sheet-anchors for
traditions that do not date themselves; verdicts graded; negatives published.

**The hypothesis under test** (Atharva's stated position, tested — not assumed,
not dismissed): *Thomas the apostle came to India.*

**Verdict key:** ATTESTED (a dated record directly establishes it) ·
CORROBORATED (independent records converge) · PLAUSIBLE–UNPROVEN (consistent
with all dated evidence, established by none) · LATE (the claim's first written
form is centuries younger than the claim) · NO BITE (the dated record does not
touch the claim at all).

## Headline results

- **Gospel of Thomas, composition:** c. **70–140 CE** for the collection
  substantially as we have it, over a sayings core that may reach back to
  c. 50–100. Hard ceiling: Greek copies were circulating in Egypt by shortly
  after 200 CE. Any date after c. 200 is excluded by the papyri.
- **China:** the Chinese record dates the Thomas-*tradition's* eastern carrier —
  the Church of the East — with spine-grade precision (arrival 635, edict 638,
  stele 781). It contains **no Thomas text, no Thomas attribution, and no mention
  of Thomas at all** in any surviving Chinese Christian or Manichaean document.
  Chinese dating bites on the tradition's *transmission*, 600–800 years after
  the events claimed. On the 1st century it has **NO BITE**.
- **India, Strand 1 (Northwest / Gondophares):** PLAUSIBLE–UNPROVEN — the
  strongest strand. The Acts of Thomas names a real, datable, otherwise
  almost-lost king (Gondophares, r. c. 20–46 CE) *and* his brother Gad.
- **India, Strand 2 (South / Malabar–Mylapore):** the *community* is ATTESTED
  from c. 550; the *52 CE itinerary* is LATE — its first written form is
  16th-century.
- **India, Strand 3 (community anchors):** FIRM for a Persian-linked church in
  India by c. 550 and a chartered church at Kollam by 849. Silent on the founder.

# PART A — THE GOSPEL OF THOMAS: WHAT ACTUALLY DATES IT

## A.1 The physical witnesses

| Witness | Content | Date | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P.Oxy 5575** (publ. Aug 2023, *Oxyrhynchus Papyri* LXXXVII) | A syncretistic sayings fragment interweaving Matthew 6 / Luke 12 material with Thomas 27 ("fast from the world…") and probably Thomas 63 | **late 2nd / early 3rd c.** (editors Wallace, Fish, Holmes, independently) | Palaeography |
| **P.Oxy 1** | Logia 26–33 + 77b, from a codex leaf | **shortly after 200 CE** | Palaeography |
| **P.Oxy 655** | Logia 24, 36–39 | **c. 200–250** | Palaeography |
| **P.Oxy 654** | Prologue + logia 1–7, from a roll | **mid-3rd c.** (some: mid-to-late 3rd) | Palaeography |
| **Nag Hammadi Codex II,2** | The complete Gospel, in Coptic (translation from Greek) | codex **c. 340 CE** | Codicology; see radiocarbon context below |

Three separate Greek copies of the same sayings collection were in circulation
at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt in the 3rd century — the text was already a *book*, not
a local curiosity, by then. P.Oxy 5575 pushes attested Thomasine *material*
into the late 2nd century, and is doubly interesting for the spine's purposes:
by c. 200 the Thomas sayings were already being copied *interleaved with*
Matthew and Luke — the "separate early Thomas" picture and the "late derivative
Thomas" picture both have to accommodate a copyist who treated them as one
stream.

Radiocarbon context for Nag Hammadi: the codices' manufacture is independently
bracketed — Codex VII's cartonnage (recycled papyrus stiffening) contains dated
documents, the latest from **348 CE**, giving a terminus post quem for that
codex's binding; radiocarbon assay of Codex I (Lundhaug et al., 2021) returned
a 99.7% range of **241–387 CE**, with a combined estimate of c. 308–387. The
Coptic Thomas (NHC II) sits in the same mid-4th-century production milieu.
None of this dates *composition* — it dates the surviving complete copy.

## A.2 Patristic mentions — TAQs for circulation

- **Hippolytus, *Refutation of All Heresies* 5.7.20 (c. 222–235 CE):** quotes
  the Naassene sect's use of a "Gospel according to Thomas," including a saying
  resembling logion 4 ("…find me in children of seven years… hidden in the
  fourteenth aeon"). This is the earliest *named* citation. Note the layering:
  Hippolytus is excerpting a Naassene source that is itself older than the
  *Refutation* — the Gospel was in sectarian circulation before c. 222.
- **Origen, *Homilies on Luke* 1 (c. 233 CE):** lists a "Gospel according to
  Thomas" among the heterodox gospels the church does not accept — bare title,
  no description, but proof the book was known by name in Alexandria/Caesarea.
- **Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.25.6 (c. 324 CE):** files the Gospel
  of Thomas among the spurious works "put forward by heretics under the names
  of apostles."
- **Cyril of Jerusalem, *Catecheses* 6.31 (c. 350 CE):** warns against a Gospel
  of Thomas he says was written by a disciple of Mani. The attribution is a
  conflation (see Part B on the Manichaean Thomas material), but it shows the
  title's currency — and its Manichaean association — by the mid-4th century.
- The Stichometry of Nicephorus assigns the Gospel 1,300 lines — a catalogue
  fossil of the same recognition.

**What these force:** a book called the Gospel of Thomas, containing sayings
of the type we possess, existed and circulated by the early 3rd century at the
very latest, and was already old enough to have a sectarian user-community
(Naassenes) and a heresiological reputation.

## A.3 The composition debate, honestly mapped

**Early camp — core c. 50–100 (Valantasis: first stratum 30–60, composition
100–110; the Koester/Crossan/Pagels line).** Rests on: (i) *genre* — a sayings
collection with no narrative and no passion account matches the form of Q, the
hypothesised sayings source behind Matthew/Luke, so Thomas could be a sibling
of Q rather than a descendant of the synoptics; (ii) a substantial set of
sayings whose Thomas form looks *less* developed than the synoptic parallel
(no redactional fingerprints of Matthew or Luke); (iii) no trace of a developed
church order, creed, or post-70 institutional world.

**Mainstream camp — c. 90–140 (the majority position).** Rests on: (i) a
minority of sayings that *do* appear to presuppose synoptic redaction (Thomas
knows Matthew's or Luke's edited form, not just the shared tradition); (ii)
the collection's editorial layering — catchword-linked clusters and a coherent
ascetic theology that fit late-1st/early-2nd-century Syrian Christianity better
than the 50s; (iii) the sheer time needed for a sayings tradition to be
collected, titled, and ascribed to "Didymus Judas Thomas."

**Late camp — post-170 (Perrin's Diatessaron thesis; Craig Evans: not before
175–180).** Rests on alleged dependence on Tatian's *Diatessaron* (c. 170):
if Thomas's parallels follow Tatian's harmonised wording, Thomas must postdate
the harmony. Perrin's version adds a Syriac original composed in Edessa. This
remains a minority position — the Diatessaronic parallels are contested case
by case — and it is squeezed hard from above by P.Oxy 5575 and P.Oxy 1: a
composition c. 180 leaves very little time for the text to reach Egypt, be
copied repeatedly, and be excerpted into a syncretistic florilegium by c. 200.
**Porter's c. 250 date is an outlier effectively falsified by the papyri** —
three Greek copies cannot postdate their own composition.

**Language and origin:** the majority holds a Greek original (the Oxyrhynchus
fragments are independent Greek witnesses, not retroversions; the Coptic is a
translation *from* Greek). A Syriac/Edessene origin is argued from the
"Didymus Judas Thomas" framing (Thomas's cult centre was Edessa) and possible
Semitic wordplay in individual sayings — suggestive of the *tradition's*
Syrian home, not proof of the *document's* language.

## A.4 Verdict — Part A

> **Composition interval: c. 70–140 CE** for the Gospel of Thomas substantially
> as we have it, over a sayings core plausibly reaching c. 50–100.
>
> Forced from below by the developed collection form and the sayings'
> engagement with the synoptic-era tradition; forced from above by P.Oxy 1
> (copy shortly after 200), P.Oxy 5575 (Thomasine sayings already interwoven
> with Matthew/Luke by the late 2nd century), and the Naassene quotation
> excerpted by Hippolytus (before c. 222–235). The honest error bars: the
> *core* could be a generation earlier than 70; the *collection* cannot be
> later than c. 200 and is very unlikely to be later than c. 150.

# PART B — THE CHINA SWEEP: CAN CHINESE DATING BITE ANYWHERE?

## B.1 The Xi'an Stele — what it actually attests

Erected **4 February 781 CE** (Tang, Jianzhong 2 — the date is on the stone),
at the Daqin temple in Chang'an; composed by the priest Jingjing (Syriac name:
Adam), calligraphy by Lü Xiuyan; ~1,756 Chinese characters plus some seventy
Syriac names of clergy; the Syriac lines name Catholicos Hnanishoʿ and
Yazdbozid, a priest whose family came from Balkh. Unearthed 1623–1625.

The stone records, as the church's own history:

- **635 CE (Zhenguan 9):** the missionary **Alopen** arrives at Chang'an from
  Daqin (the Roman Orient) carrying scriptures and images; received at court;
  the scriptures are translated in the imperial library.
- **638 CE (Zhenguan 12):** Taizong's **edict** approves the religion's
  propagation and authorises a temple in the Yining ward of Chang'an with
  twenty-one monks.
- The Messiah "left **twenty-seven books**" — the church's canon gesture.
- A century and a half of imperial patronage, persecution (the 845 Huichang
  suppression looms just after the stone), and clergy lists down to 781.

**What it does not do: name Thomas.** Not once. The stele's sacred history
runs from creation through the Incarnation to Alopen; the apostle of the East
is absent from his own church's foundation monument.

The Church of the East's *own framing*, from its Syriac tradition, is
different and must be stated fairly: that church traced its apostolic origin
to **Thomas together with Addai (Thaddeus) and Mari**. The Syriac *Doctrine of
the Apostles* (received form c. 250) assigns "India and all its countries" to
Judas Thomas; the *Didascalia* (late 3rd c.) is similar; Ephrem (d. 373) sings
Thomas's Indian mission and the translation of his relics to Edessa. By the
stele's century the Catholicos at Seleucia-Ctesiphon claimed jurisdiction
over a metropolitan province of China. So the 635 mission *understood itself*
as Thomas's church arriving in China — but that self-understanding is a
tradition about the 1st century, recorded in the 3rd–8th, and the Chinese
record dates only the arrival, never the origin.

## B.2 The Jingjiao corpus and the Zunjing catalogue — the Thomas search

The surviving Tang-dynasty Jingjiao corpus in Chinese is small and fully
catalogued: roughly seven to nine authentic documents (the Stele; the
*Xuting Mishisuo Jing* / Book of Jesus-Messiah; the *Yishen Lun*, internally
dated by some scholars to 642; the *Sanwei Mengdu Zan* and the *Zunjing*,
both preserved in the Dunhuang manuscript Pelliot chinois 3847; the *Zhixuan
Anle Jing*; the *Xuanyuan Zhiben Jing*, also carved on the Luoyang Jingjiao
pillar of 814/815; the *Dasheng Tongzhen Guifa Zan*). The Alopen-era texts
draw their gospel material from Matthew and Acts.

The decisive witness is the **Zunjing** ("Honoured Books," late 8th century —
Jingjing's own lifetime, preserved in P.3847). It is the church's own
catalogue of itself: it states that the Daqin religion possesses **530 books**
written on pattra leaves, of which Jingjing translated about thirty, and it
lists **35 titles**. In the published identifications of that list (Saeki;
Li Tang, *Artifact, Text, Context*, 2020), the titles resolve to: the Psalter,
the Gospels (*Ewangeliyon*), an Acts-type work, the Apostles, the Pauline
epistles, Zechariah, the Law of Moses, lectionary and liturgical books
(Qeryānā, Gloria/Teshbohtā, service books). Its diptych of honoured persons
names prophets and apostles — David, Hosea, Peter and Paul are the examples
the literature gives.

**Finding: no Gospel of Thomas, no Acts of Thomas, and no Thomas-titled or
Thomas-attributed work appears among the 35 titles as identified, and no
Thomas saying has been identified in any surviving Chinese Jingjiao text.**
This is a *verified absence* in the spine's sense: the corpus is small, the
catalogue is the church's own, and the catalogue's whole purpose was to list
what the church held. If a Thomas book had reached Tang China in Chinese,
the Zunjing is exactly where it would have to appear.

Caveat, stated plainly: 530 claimed books, ~35 translated, a handful survive.
Absence from the *translated and surviving* corpus is not proof that no
Thomas text ever sat untranslated on a Chang'an shelf. It is proof that
Thomas material formed no part of the Jingjiao church's *working Chinese
library* — which is the claim that matters for dating anything.

## B.3 The Manichaean route — searched, and the distinction that matters

Three different "Thomas" objects must not be conflated:

1. **The Gospel of Thomas** — the sayings gospel of Part A.
2. **The Acts of Thomas** — the Syriac romance of Part C.
3. **The Psalms of Thomas** — twenty psalms appended to the Coptic Manichaean
   Psalm-Book from Medinet Madi (ed. Allberry 1938, pp. 203–227), probably
   translated from an Eastern Aramaic original of the 3rd century. Their
   "Thomas" is genuinely contested: the apostle, Mani's disciple Thomas, or
   the "divine twin" figure of Syrian religion. Säve-Söderbergh (1949) showed
   deep Mandaean parallels; Church & Stroumsa argue for a pre-Manichaean
   Syrian Thomasine origin — the same milieu as the Gospel and the Acts.

The Gospel itself demonstrably circulated in Manichaean hands — **in Egypt**:
Wolf-Peter Funk documented actual quotations from the Gospel of Thomas in
Coptic Manichaica. Cyril of Jerusalem's claim (c. 350) that a disciple of Mani
wrote a "Gospel of Thomas" is the patristic echo of exactly this association.

Manichaeism's eastern road is well dated: a Persian bishop (fu-duo-dan) at the
Tang court in **694** (Fozu Tongji); the *Compendium* of Mani's teaching
compiled at Xuanzong's order, internally dated **16 July 731**; the Hymnscroll
(S.2659, late 8th c., translated via Parthian at Turfan); the Traité fragment
from Dunhuang. **But no Thomas psalm, no Thomas gospel fragment, and no Thomas
attribution has been identified in any Chinese or Turfan Manichaean
manuscript.** The Thomas material stayed in the Egyptian/Coptic branch of
Manichaeism as far as all surviving evidence shows. The road existed; the
Thomas cargo, on present evidence, never travelled it.

One more eastern library checked: the Church of the East monastery at
**Bulayïq near Turfan** yielded ~1,100 fragments (9th–13th c.) in Syriac,
Sogdian and Old Turkic — Bibles, lectionaries, liturgy, and hagiography
(Persian martyrs, St George, the Sleepers of Ephesus, St Pethion in
Sims-Williams' 2012 catalogue register). **No fragment of the Acts of Thomas
has been identified in the published catalogues.** The Acts was beloved in
Syriac Christianity and travelled widely in the West of the church's world;
its absence from the identified Turfan contents is a survival statement, not
proof it never reached Central Asia — but it means Central Asia currently
provides no dated witness to it either.

## B.4 Tang records and the pre-635 question

- **Institutional record:** Jingjiao temples were first officially classed as
  *Bosi si* ("Persian temples"); an edict of **745 (Tianbao 4)** renamed them
  *Daqin si* ("Daqin temples") in the two capitals and all prefectures — the
  text is preserved in Tang sources. The *Tang Huiyao* (fasc. 49, compiled
  961) carries the official account, including the 638 edict.
- **Chang'an:** fully attested (the stele's own temple; the Daqin temple site
  at Zhouzhi). **Guangzhou:** Persian and Arab merchant presence is
  Tang-attested (including the 758 sack of the port by Arab and Persian
  traders in the Tang annals); a specifically *Christian* church there in
  Tang times is claimed in later tradition but is **not established** by a
  Tang-dated document.
- **Pre-635:** Chinese sources record Daqin embassies (166, 226, 285 CE) and
  more than thirty Persian embassies between 455 and 651 — diplomacy and
  trade, with no religious content named. **No pre-635 Chinese text mentions
  Christians, a church, or Thomas.** Searched; not found. (Modern claims of a
  Han-dynasty Thomas mission to China circulate in apologetic literature;
  they rest on no Chinese document and are not evidence.)
- **Second wave:** under the Mongols the Church of the East returned
  (the Yelikewen of Yuan records; Rabban Bar Sauma's embassy west, 1287;
  Mar Yaballaha III, a monk from Khanbaliq, became Catholicos in 1281).
  Even at this maximum eastern reach, no Thomas text in Chinese appears.

## B.5 Verdicts — Part B

| Claimed connection | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Alopen's arrival 635 / edict 638 / stele 781 | **ATTESTED** — spine-grade dated events |
| The arriving church understood itself as Thomas's church | **ATTESTED as that church's tradition** (Syriac sources, 3rd c. onward) — a tradition *about* Thomas, not a record *of* him |
| Gospel of Thomas in Chinese, any date | **NO BITE** — verified absence from the Zunjing's 35 titles and the whole surviving Jingjiao corpus |
| Thomas material via Manichaeism into China | **NO BITE** — Gospel-of-Thomas quotations are Coptic/Egyptian only; no Thomas item identified in Chinese/Turfan Manichaean remains |
| Acts of Thomas in Central Asia (Turfan) | **NO BITE** on present catalogues — no identified fragment |
| Any pre-635 Chinese mention of Christians or Thomas | **NO BITE** — none exists in the searched record |
| Psalms of Thomas as evidence about the apostle | **LATE / not the apostle's** — a 3rd-c. Manichaean-cycle psalm collection, attribution contested; do not conflate with the Gospel |

**The composition-vs-transmission line, kept sharp:** Chinese dates pin the
Thomas tradition's *carrier* at 635, 638, 745, 781 and its Central Asian
library at the 9th–13th centuries. Every one of those dates is a
*transmission* date, 600–1,200 years downstream of the events the tradition
claims. **They do not touch the 1st century at all** — and the one witness
that could have carried a Thomas text east in datable form, the Zunjing
catalogue, affirmatively lists a library with no Thomas in it.

# PART C — THE INDIA HYPOTHESIS, STRAND BY STRAND

## C.1 Strand 1 — The Northwest: the Acts of Thomas and King Gondophares

**The text.** The *Acts of Thomas* (full title: Acts of Judas Thomas) was
composed in **Syriac**, most plausibly in the Edessa orbit, in the **early
3rd century** — scholarly datings cluster c. 200–250 (Klijn's edition: the
present form c. 225–250; several place the core at the turn of the century,
c. 180–230). It is the earliest surviving narrative of a Thomas mission to
India: Thomas, sold as a slave-carpenter to Habban, agent of **King
Gudnaphar (Gondophares)**, sails to India, builds the king a palace (in
heaven, by almsgiving), and converts the royal household; a second cycle
takes him to the realm of King Mazdai, where he is martyred. The *Hymn of
the Pearl*, embedded in the Acts, may be an older, separate composition.

**The king is real.** Gondophares (Old Persian Vindafarnah) is an
Indo-Parthian king known *only* from three sources: the Acts, his extensive
silver and copper coinage, and one inscription. Encyclopaedia Iranica
(Bivar): reign **20–46 CE** over Drangiana, Arachosia and the Punjab.

**The inscription.** The Takht-i-Bahi Kharoshthi inscription is dated to
**year 103 of an era and Gondophares' 26th regnal year**. On the standard
identification of that era with the Azes era — which Bivar states is "now
known to be identical" with the Vikrama era, year 1 = **57 BCE** — year 103
= **46 CE**, and accession falls c. **20 CE**. **Era assumption, stated as
the spine requires:** the inscription does not name its era. If the era is
not Azes/Vikrama, the absolute date moves with it. Robert Senior's
alternative numismatic chronology further argues the inscription's king may
be a *later* Gondophares than the coin-king of c. 20–46. The standard
reading (46 CE) is the majority position, not a certainty.

**The core question, put at full strength for Atharva's side:** the Acts
does not merely name Gondophares. It names his **brother Gad** as a royal
figure who dies and is raised — and "Gadana/Gad" appears among the
subordinate names on Gondophares' own coinage (Iranica). Gondophares
appears in **no Greek or Roman literary source** — no historian, geographer
or chronicle available to a 3rd-century Edessene writer preserves his name.
Until Cunningham's coin reports of **1854**, the Acts of Thomas was the
*only literary witness on earth* to this king's existence. A romancer
inventing a setting reached, somehow, for a real king of exactly the right
region (the Indo-Parthian northwest), of exactly the right generation
(a contemporary of the apostolic age), with a correctly named brother —
for a king whom classical literature had entirely forgotten and only his
coins remembered.

**The strongest counter-argument, also at full strength:** those coins are
precisely the problem. Gondophares' coinage was struck in quantity and
circulated across exactly the trade world — Gandhara, the Punjab, the
routes to Mesopotamia — that an Edessene author inhabited; coins carry
their legends for generations. Worse for the identification, **"Gondophares"
became a dynastic title**: Gondophares IV Sases (mid-to-late 1st c.) assumed
the name as supreme Indo-Parthian rulers did, as "Augustus" and "Caesar"
outlived their first bearers — so the name remained *current on money* for
a century and more after 46 CE. An author c. 220 could have taken the name
(and a brother's name from the same coin legends) from the monetary
landscape without any mission tradition behind it. Add the genre prior: the
Acts is a romance containing talking animals and serial resurrections, and
its second half abandons Gondophares entirely for the unhistorical Mazdai.
And the earliest "division of the world" tradition cuts the other way —
Origen (via Eusebius, *HE* 3.1) allots **Parthia** to Thomas and India to
Bartholomew.

**Assessment.** The double hit (king + brother, right place, right
generation, name absent from all classical literature) is more than a
romancer needed and more than pure invention usually manages; the
coin-circulation route explains the *name's availability* but not obviously
the *pairing and placement*. What the strand proves: a Syriac tradition
placing Thomas at Gondophares' court existed by c. 200–250 and was built
around genuine 1st-century prosopography. What it cannot prove: that the
meeting happened.

> **Strand 1 grade: PLAUSIBLE–UNPROVEN** — the strongest strand in the file.
> *Would raise it:* any pre-200 CE document or inscription independent of
> the Acts — Indian, Parthian or Syriac — naming Christians, or Thomas, in
> Gondophares' realm; a 1st–2nd-century Christian artefact in Gandhara.
> *Would lower it:* a pre-Acts written source (merchant handbook, onomasticon)
> circulating "Gudnaphar and Gad" as stock eastern-king names in Mesopotamia.

## C.2 Strand 2 — The South: Malabar and Mylapore

**The tradition's own claim:** Thomas landed at Muziris (Kodungallur/
Cranganore) in **52 CE**, founded seven churches on the Malabar coast,
crossed to the Coromandel coast, and was martyred at **Mylapore** in
**72 CE**, where his tomb is venerated to this day.

**First attestations in writing, in order** — the ladder matters more than
any rung:

1. **c. 180–190 — Eusebius, *HE* 5.10:** Pantaenus finds Christians in
   "India" who say Bartholomew left them a Hebrew Matthew. The apostle is
   **Bartholomew**, not Thomas, and "India" in this period can denote
   South Arabia. Christians *somewhere* east — nothing more.
2. **c. 200–250 — the Acts of Thomas:** Thomas in "India" — but the Acts'
   geography is the Indo-Parthian northwest plus Mazdai's unnamed kingdom.
   It names **no Malabar place, no Muziris, no Mylapore**.
3. **c. 250 — *Doctrine of the Apostles* / *Didascalia*:** the Syriac
   allotment tradition now assigns India to Thomas. The *assignment* is
   3rd-century; it is about India generally.
4. **before 373 — Ephrem the Syrian's hymns:** Thomas evangelised India,
   was martyred there, and his relics were brought to Edessa (the merchant
   Habban motif). The earliest clear Thomas–India–martyrdom complex outside
   the Acts itself. Still no southern itinerary.
5. **384 — Egeria's pilgrimage diary:** Thomas's martyrium at Edessa,
   relics present. Confirms the Edessa cult, not the Indian site.
6. **c. 547–550 — Cosmas Indicopleustes, *Christian Topography*:** a church
   "in the country called **Male**, where the pepper grows" (the Malabar
   coast), a bishop at **Kalliana** (Kalyan) "appointed from Persia," a
   church with Persian-appointed clergy in **Taprobane** (Sri Lanka).
   **Christians in south India: ATTESTED** — as a Persian-linked church.
   Thomas is not named as founder.
7. **c. 590 — Gregory of Tours, *Glory of the Martyrs*:** Thomas was
   martyred in India, where a monastery and temple mark the cult site;
   his remains were later translated to Edessa. First Latin witness to a
   Thomas *cult site in India* — the place is not named.
8. **6th–10th c. — the "St Thomas crosses":** stone crosses with Pahlavi
   inscriptions on both coasts, including the Mylapore mount cross —
   Persian-linked Christian presence on the Coromandel coast in the early
   medieval centuries.
9. **883 — the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle:** King Alfred sends Sigehelm and
   Æthelstan with alms "to Rome — and also to India, to St Thomas and St
   Bartholomew." William of Malmesbury (12th c.) adds that Sighelm
   returned with gems and spices. The Indian Thomas shrine was famous
   enough in 9th-century Wessex to be the object of a royal vow. It attests
   the shrine's *fame*, at one remove.
10. **c. 1292–1298 — Marco Polo:** the tomb of St Thomas in **Maabar**
    (the Coromandel coast), in a small town identifiable as Mylapore,
    venerated by Christians and Saracens alike; pilgrims carry away earth
    from the martyrdom spot as medicine. **First unambiguous written pinning
    of the tomb to Mylapore.**
11. **16th century — Portuguese records and the local tradition in written
    form:** the full itinerary — 52 CE landing at Muziris, the seven
    churches, the 72 CE Mylapore martyrdom — is written down for the first
    time, by Portuguese observers recording what the St Thomas Christians
    told them, and in local song-cycles (Margam Kali, Thomma Parvam) whose
    surviving written forms are 17th–18th-century. The tradition may be far
    older than its paper trail. The paper trail is what a dating instrument
    can grade, and it begins here.
12. **Thomas of Cana (Knai Thoma):** the tradition of an East Syriac
    merchant-bishop arriving with settlers, dated by the community to
    345 CE or to the 8th century. No contemporary evidence; graded as
    legend possibly preserving a kernel of a real merchant migration.

**Trade-route plausibility — filed separately, as required.** A 1st-century
sea passage to Malabar was not merely possible but routine: the *Periplus
of the Erythraean Sea* (mid-1st c.) describes Muziris in detail — ships
from Arabia and the Greek world, imports headed by "a great quantity of
coin"; Pliny calls Muziris the first emporium of India and complains that
India, China and Arabia drain 100 million sesterces a year from the empire;
the monsoon run from the Red Sea took roughly forty days; hoards of
Augustan and Tiberian gold and silver coins cluster in the Muziris
hinterland and across Tamil Nadu; excavations at **Pattanam** (the leading
Muziris candidate, near Kodungallur) have produced Roman amphorae,
rouletted ware, Mediterranean glass and an intaglio. A traveller from the
Roman Orient could reach Muziris in 52 CE as easily as a pepper merchant
did — **and this proves exactly nothing about whether one did.** Routine
possibility is not an attestation, and this file does not spend it as one.

> **Strand 2 grade: SPLIT.** The south-Indian Christian *community*:
> **ATTESTED from c. 550** (Cosmas), with a Thomas tomb-cult in India by
> c. 590 (Gregory) and the tomb fixed at Mylapore by c. 1298 (Polo). The
> *52 CE / 72 CE itinerary*: **LATE** — no written attestation before the
> 16th century; between the claim (52 CE) and its first written form stand
> roughly 1,450 years of silence in every Indian, Persian, Greek and Syriac
> source, in a region whose copper-plate habit recorded far smaller things.
> *Would raise it:* a single pre-Portuguese Indian document — inscription,
> plate, coin legend — naming Thomas; a datable 1st–3rd-century Christian
> artefact on the Malabar coast. *Would lower it:* demonstration that the
> Mylapore cult site postdates the Portuguese arrival (currently the
> opposite is the scholarly default — Polo precedes the Portuguese by two
> centuries).

## C.3 Strand 3 — The community anchors

What the dated evidence establishes, and only that:

- **c. 550 (Cosmas):** an organised church in India — clergy, bishops
  ordained in Persia, congregations on the Malabar coast, at Kalyan, in Sri
  Lanka and Socotra. The Indian church's supply line ran to **Persia**, not
  to any local apostolic succession Cosmas mentions.
- **c. 825 (tradition) / 849 (document): Mar Sabor and Mar Proth.** Kerala
  tradition brings two East Syriac bishops, Sabor and Proth, to Kollam
  c. 823–825 with a merchant migration. The document is the **Quilon
  (Tarisapalli) copper plates of 849/850**: Ayyan Adikal, chief of Kollam,
  grants land and privileges to the Tarisapalli church under the merchant
  **Maruvan Sapir Iso**, in the 5th regnal year of the Chera king Sthanu
  Ravi; the witness plate carries signatures in **Pahlavi**, Kufic Arabic
  and Judeo-Persian (the Pahlavi signatures edited by Cereti, 2009).
  Precision, because the spine grades documents not traditions: the plates
  name Maruvan Sapir Iso; the identification of the migration's leaders
  with "Mar Sabor and Mar Proth" is the community's tradition, consistent
  with the plates but not stated in them.
- **What these prove:** an East Syriac-linked, Persian-clerried,
  royally chartered Christian church existed in India by the mid-6th
  century and on the Malabar coast by 849 at the latest. **What they do
  not prove:** anything about the church's founder. A church attested in
  550 is fully consistent with a 1st-century foundation, a 3rd-century
  merchant-chaplaincy origin, or a 4th–5th-century Persian mission — the
  anchors date the *institution*, and the institution's own foundation
  story is Strand 2's late-attested tradition, not Strand 3's evidence.

> **Strand 3 grade: FIRM** — for the proposition "a Persian-linked church
> existed in India by c. 550 and at Kollam by 849." It is the floor under
> the whole file: whatever happened in the 1st century, *something* was
> there by the 6th, and it faced Persia.
